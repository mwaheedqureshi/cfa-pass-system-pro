# Analysis of Long-Term Assets

Long-term assets create benefits over many periods, but accounting recognizes only assets that satisfy recognition and measurement rules. Analysts must understand what was capitalized, what was expensed, how carrying amounts change, and how estimates affect comparability.

## 1. Intangible assets by origin

An identifiable intangible asset lacks physical substance and is separable or arises from contractual or legal rights. Purchased intangibles are recorded at cost, including directly attributable preparation costs. In a business combination, identifiable intangibles are recognized separately at fair value; the residual purchase price over the fair value of identifiable net assets is goodwill.

### Worked example 1: separate purchase

A patent costs 500 plus 20 of legal registration fees. Initial carrying amount is 520. General training and launch advertising are expensed because they do not prepare the patent itself for use.

### Worked example 2: business combination

Consideration is 1,200 and fair value of identifiable net assets is 980, including a previously unrecorded customer relationship of 160. Goodwill is 220; the customer relationship is not buried in goodwill.

### Original table 1: origin determines reporting

| Origin | Initial treatment | Key analytical issue |
|---|---|---|
| Separately purchased | Capitalize cost | Useful life and amortization assumptions |
| Internally developed | Expense or capitalize according to phase/framework | Comparability of investment and profit |
| Business combination | Recognize identifiable assets at fair value; residual is goodwill | Valuation estimates and later impairment |

IFRS expenses research. Development expenditure is capitalized only after technical feasibility, intent and ability to complete/use or sell, probable benefits, adequate resources, and reliable measurement are demonstrated. US GAAP generally expenses research and development, with specified exceptions for software. Software to be sold is capitalized after technological feasibility; internal-use software is capitalized after the project reaches the qualifying application-development stage.

### Worked example 3: IFRS development threshold

A project spends 12 per month. Recognition criteria are demonstrated on 1 July. The first 72 is expensed and the final 72 is capitalized; earlier expense is not reinstated.

### Worked example 4: analyst normalization

Company A capitalizes 40 of qualifying development while Company B expenses economically similar spending. Before adjustment, A reports higher assets, equity, profit, CFO and CFI outflow, while B reports lower profit and CFO. Total cash flow is unchanged.

### Original diagram 2: internal-project decision flow

```text
Research phase -> expense
Development phase -> criteria all met? -> no: expense
                                      \-> yes: capitalize prospective costs
```

Finite-life intangibles are amortized over useful life and tested for impairment when indicators exist. Indefinite-life intangibles are not amortized but are tested at least annually. “Indefinite” does not mean infinite; reassess the life classification.

## 2. Impairment

Under IFRS, recoverable amount is the higher of fair value less costs of disposal and value in use. If carrying amount exceeds recoverable amount, recognize the difference as impairment. Under US GAAP for long-lived assets held for use, first compare carrying amount with undiscounted cash flows. Only if the screen fails is loss measured as carrying amount minus fair value.

### Worked example 5: different framework outcomes

Carrying amount is 18,000, undiscounted cash flows 19,000, value in use 16,000, and fair value less costs 17,000. IFRS records a 1,000 loss. US GAAP passes the undiscounted-cash-flow screen and records no loss.

### Original table 3: impairment comparison

| Question | IFRS | US GAAP held for use |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger | Indicator; annual test for indefinite-life assets/goodwill | Indicator; annual test for indefinite-life assets/goodwill |
| Recoverability | Carrying amount vs higher of value in use and FVLCD | Carrying amount vs undiscounted cash flows |
| Measurement | Carrying amount minus recoverable amount | Carrying amount minus fair value after failed screen |
| Reversal | Permitted for assets other than goodwill, within ceiling | Prohibited for assets held for use |

An impairment lowers assets, profit, and equity. It is non-cash, so indirect-method CFO adds it back. Lower future carrying value usually lowers future depreciation or amortization, raising later profit and asset turnover mechanically.

### Worked example 6: ratio effects

A 100 impairment reduces assets and pretax income by 100. Debt-to-assets rises if debt is unchanged; current ratio is normally unaffected for a non-current asset; future asset turnover and margins may rise without better economics.

### Worked example 7: IFRS reversal ceiling

An asset written from 90 to 60 would have carried at 75 without impairment. If recoverable amount later becomes 82, reversal is limited to 15, producing a 75 carrying amount.

### Original diagram 4: impairment timeline

```text
Loss now: assets/profit/equity down, no operating cash outflow
Later: lower depreciation -> reported profit up
Possible IFRS recovery -> reversal ceiling = no-impairment carrying amount
```

Held-for-sale assets are available for immediate sale and sale is highly probable. They are measured at the lower of carrying amount and fair value less costs to sell and cease to be depreciated or amortized.

## 3. Derecognition

On sale, gain or loss equals proceeds minus carrying amount on the disposal date. Remove both historical cost and accumulated depreciation. The entire cash receipt is an investing inflow; the gain or loss is removed from CFO under the indirect method because it is already included in net income.

### Worked example 8: asset sale

Equipment cost 100, accumulated depreciation is 45, and proceeds are 62. Carrying amount is 55 and gain is 7. Investing cash inflow is 62, not 7.

### Original table 5: sale entries and statement links

| Component | Amount | Location |
|---|---:|---|
| Proceeds | 62 | Investing cash inflow |
| Carrying amount removed | 55 | Balance sheet derecognition |
| Gain | 7 | Income statement; deducted in indirect CFO reconciliation |

## 4. Presentation, disclosures, and analytical estimates

Disclosures identify measurement bases, depreciation/amortization methods, useful lives or rates, gross carrying amounts, accumulated depreciation/amortization and impairment, additions, disposals, acquisitions, revaluations, impairments, and reconciliations from opening to closing balances. Analysts should connect the balance sheet, income statement, cash-flow statement, notes, and management commentary.

### Original checklist 6: disclosure audit

1. Reconcile opening carrying amount to closing carrying amount.
2. Separate organic capital expenditure from acquisitions and currency effects.
3. Examine useful lives, residual values, and methods against peers.
4. Identify impairment assumptions, cash-generating units, discount rates, and sensitivity.
5. Distinguish maintenance from expansion investment.
6. Check whether gains on disposal or reduced depreciation boosted profit.

For historical-cost PPE with straight-line depreciation and zero salvage value, disclosures support estimates of total useful life, average age, and remaining life. These are approximations: mixed asset ages, land, different methods, fully depreciated assets, acquisitions, disposals, and foreign exchange can distort them.

### Worked example 9: estimated lives

Gross PPE is 240, accumulated depreciation 144, net PPE 96, and annual depreciation 12. Estimated useful life is 20 years, age is 12 years, and remaining life is 8 years.

### Worked example 10: percentage of life elapsed

Accumulated depreciation of 144 divided by gross depreciable PPE of 240 is 60%. A mature asset base may imply upcoming replacement spending, but composition and maintenance history matter.

### Original table 7: disclosure-derived estimates

| Estimate | Relationship | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Total useful life | Gross depreciable PPE / depreciation | Longer life lowers annual expense, all else equal |
| Average age | Accumulated depreciation / depreciation | Higher age suggests older asset base |
| Remaining life | Net PPE / depreciation | Approximate years before current book value is consumed |
| Life elapsed | Accumulated depreciation / gross PPE | Replacement-cycle indicator |

Fixed-asset turnover equals revenue divided by average net PPE. Higher turnover may reflect efficiency, but also old or heavily impaired assets, leasing, outsourcing, inflation differences, or conservative capitalization. Capital expenditure divided by depreciation is a rough reinvestment indicator: above one may suggest capacity expansion or replacement-cost inflation, not automatically value creation.

### Worked example 11: fixed-asset turnover

Revenue is 500 and beginning/end net PPE are 190 and 210. Average net PPE is 200 and turnover is 2.5 times.

### Worked example 12: reinvestment ratio

Capital expenditure is 66 and depreciation is 50. The ratio is 1.32. Investigate whether spending maintains capacity, expands it, or merely reflects higher replacement prices.

### Original matrix 8: accounting choice and analytical direction

| Choice/event | Current profit | Current assets | Later profit |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Capitalize rather than expense | Higher | Higher | Lower through amortization/depreciation |
| Longer useful life | Higher | Higher | Expense deferred |
| Impairment | Lower | Lower | Higher from lower future expense |
| Gain-producing disposal | Higher | Lower asset base | Non-recurring benefit |

## Formula recap

1. Goodwill = consideration − fair value of identifiable net assets.
2. IFRS recoverable amount = max(value in use, fair value less costs of disposal).
3. IFRS impairment loss = max(0, carrying amount − recoverable amount).
4. US GAAP screen: impaired only if carrying amount exceeds undiscounted cash flows; loss then equals carrying amount − fair value.
5. Disposal gain/(loss) = proceeds − carrying amount.
6. Net PPE = gross PPE − accumulated depreciation.
7. Estimated useful life = gross depreciable PPE / annual depreciation.
8. Estimated age = accumulated depreciation / annual depreciation.
9. Estimated remaining life = net depreciable PPE / annual depreciation.
10. Percentage of life elapsed = accumulated depreciation / gross depreciable PPE.
11. Fixed-asset turnover = revenue / average net PPE.
12. Reinvestment ratio = capital expenditure / depreciation expense.

The analyst's discipline is to reconstruct the asset's economic story from recognition policy, estimates, roll-forwards, impairment evidence, and cash investment—not to read a low carrying amount or high turnover as automatically favorable.
