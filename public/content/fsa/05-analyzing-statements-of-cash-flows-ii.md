# Analyzing Statements of Cash Flows II

## Learning outcomes

1. analyze and interpret both reported and common-size cash flow statements
2. calculate and interpret free cash flow to the firm, free cash flow to equity, and performance and coverage cash flow ratios

## From preparation to analysis

LM4 explained how cash flows are constructed. LM5 asks what the pattern means. A robust review proceeds from total sources and uses, through the drivers within CFO, CFI, and CFF, to common-size comparisons, free cash flow, and cash-based performance and coverage ratios.

## Section 1 — Sources, uses, and sustainability

### Original table — four-step cash-flow review

| Step | Question |
|---|---|
| 1 | What are the major sources and uses across CFO, CFI, and CFF? |
| 2 | What drives CFO, and is it persistent? |
| 3 | What drives CFI: productive investment or asset liquidation? |
| 4 | What drives CFF: external funding or returns to capital providers? |

A mature firm should normally generate positive CFO sufficient to invest and/or return capital. A growth firm may temporarily consume operating cash while building inventory and receivables, but permanent dependence on borrowing, share issuance, or asset sales is unsustainable.

### Worked example 1 — mature pattern

Orion reports CFO +$120m, CFI −$75m, and CFF −$30m. Operations fund productive investment and distributions, leaving a $15m cash increase. This is generally a healthy mature-company pattern.

### Worked example 2 — funding warning

Pioneer reports CFO −$18m, CFI +$12m from asset sales, and CFF +$20m from borrowing. Cash rises $14m, but the company is funding operating deficits by liquidation and debt. The cash increase is not evidence of operating strength.

### Worked example 3 — working-capital quality

CFO rises $25m while net income is flat because payables rise $22m. The analyst separates the supplier-financing benefit from repeatable cash generation: payables cannot grow faster than purchases forever.

### Original diagram — sustainable cash hierarchy

```text
Operations generate cash
        ↓
Maintain and expand productive capacity
        ↓
Service debt and return residual cash to owners

Warning inversion: borrowing / asset sales → fund recurring operations
```

### Worked example 4 — growth-company nuance

A young distributor has CFO −$8m because receivables rise $12m and inventory rises $9m while sales grow 60%. The pattern may be consistent with growth, but the analyst tests collections, inventory turnover, funding runway, and the path to positive CFO.

## Section 2 — Common-size cash flow statements

Two methods answer different questions:

- Percentage-of-total-inflows/outflows shows the composition of cash sources and uses.
- Percentage-of-net-revenue scales each cash-flow line to business volume and supports margins and peer comparison.

### Original table — common-size methods

| Method | Denominator | Best use |
|---|---|---|
| Inflow/outflow | Total cash inflows or total cash outflows | Source/use concentration |
| Net revenue | Net revenue | Scale-neutral trend and peer comparison |

### Worked example 5 — inflow concentration

Customer receipts are $360m, borrowing $90m, and asset-sale proceeds $50m. Total inflows are $500m; common-size shares are 72%, 18%, and 10%. If customer receipts were only 55% last year, operating sources have become more important.

### Worked example 6 — revenue scaling

Revenue is $600m, CFO $90m, capital spending $72m, and dividends $18m. Revenue-scaled amounts are CFO 15%, capex 12%, and dividends 3%. These percentages can be compared with peers regardless of absolute size.

### Original table — interpretation matrix

| Pattern | Possible interpretation | Follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| CFO/net revenue rising | Better cash conversion | Is improvement earnings-driven or working-capital-driven? |
| CFI outflow/revenue rising | Expansion or heavy replacement | Review capacity, age, and expected returns |
| Asset sales dominate inflows | Possible retrenchment | Determine recurring vs one-off disposal |
| Borrowing dominates inflows | External funding dependence | Test leverage and maturity capacity |

### Worked example 7 — normalize before comparing

An IFRS firm classifies $10m interest paid in CFF; a US GAAP peer includes it in CFO. Move the IFRS interest to CFO before comparing CFO margins. Total cash does not change, but the normalized operating comparison does.

## Section 3 — Free cash flow

FCFF is available to debt and equity investors after operating costs, taxes, and necessary fixed/working-capital investment. FCFE is available to common shareholders after fixed-capital investment and net debt cash flows.

### Original table — FCFF and FCFE bridge

| Measure | Formula | Claimants |
|---|---|---|
| FCFF from NI | NI + NCC + Int(1−t) − FCInv − WCInv | Debt and equity |
| FCFF from CFO | CFO + Int(1−t) − FCInv | Debt and equity |
| FCFE | CFO − FCInv + Net borrowing | Common equity |

### Worked example 8 — FCFF from net income

NI $50m, NCC $12m, after-tax interest $5m, fixed-capital investment $20m, and working-capital investment $7m produce **FCFF = $40m**.

### Worked example 9 — FCFF from CFO

CFO $80m, interest $10m, tax rate 40%, and FCInv $30m produce **FCFF = 80 + 10(1−0.40) − 30 = $56m**.

If IFRS interest paid was classified in financing, CFO was never reduced by it, so do not add it back again. Classification normalization is part of the calculation.

### Worked example 10 — FCFE

CFO $80m, FCInv $30m, new borrowing $15m, and principal repayment $10m give net borrowing $5m and **FCFE = $55m**. If repayments instead exceeded borrowing by $5m, FCFE would be $45m.

### Original diagram — claimant logic

```text
CFO
− fixed-capital investment
= cash before net debt flows
+ net borrowing → FCFE (equity only)
+ after-tax interest adjustment → FCFF (debt + equity)
```

## Section 4 — Cash-flow performance ratios

### Original table — performance ratios

| Ratio | Formula | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Cash flow to revenue | CFO / net revenue | Cash per sales dollar |
| Cash return on assets | CFO / average total assets | Cash per asset dollar |
| Cash return on equity | CFO / average equity | Cash per owner dollar |
| Cash to income | CFO / operating income | Cash conversion of operating profit |
| Cash flow per share | (CFO − preferred dividends) / common shares | Operating cash per common share |

### Worked example 11 — performance set

CFO $90m, revenue $600m, average assets $750m, average equity $300m, and operating income $75m produce cash flow/revenue **15%**, cash ROA **12%**, cash ROE **30%**, and cash-to-income **1.20**.

### Worked example 12 — per share

CFO $66m less $6m preferred dividends, divided by 20m common shares, gives **$3.00 cash flow per share**. This is not EPS and is not a valuation measure by itself.

## Section 5 — Cash-flow coverage ratios

### Original table — coverage ratios

| Ratio | Formula | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Debt coverage | CFO / total debt | Operating cash relative to debt |
| Interest coverage | (CFO + interest paid + taxes paid) / interest paid | Cash ability to meet interest |
| Reinvestment | CFO / cash paid for long-term assets | Internal funding of asset purchases |
| Debt payment | CFO / long-term debt repayment | Principal-payment capacity |
| Dividend payment | CFO / dividends paid | Dividend capacity |
| Investing and financing | CFO / CFI and CFF cash outflows | Coverage of investment and capital distributions |

### Worked example 13 — interest coverage

CFO $70m, interest paid $10m, and taxes paid $20m produce cash interest coverage **10.0x**: (70 + 10 + 20) / 10.

### Worked example 14 — coverage dashboard

CFO $120m, debt $400m, long-term asset purchases $80m, principal repayments $30m, dividends $24m, and total CFI/CFF outflows $150m produce debt coverage **0.30**, reinvestment **1.50x**, debt payment **4.0x**, dividend payment **5.0x**, and investing/financing coverage **0.80x**. The firm covers individual commitments well but not all investment and financing outflows from current CFO together.

## Analyst workflow

1. Identify total sources and uses and the business-life-cycle context.
2. Separate recurring CFO drivers from temporary working-capital releases.
3. Interpret investment outflows using expected economic return, not sign alone.
4. Assess dependence on or distributions to capital providers.
5. Common-size by inflows/outflows and by revenue.
6. Normalize classification policies.
7. Calculate FCFF/FCFE and the complete ratio family using consistent definitions.
8. Compare trends and peers; never interpret one ratio in isolation.

## Common mistakes

- Calling every negative CFI unfavorable.
- Treating a cash increase funded by borrowing as operating strength.
- Adding after-tax interest to IFRS CFO when interest paid is already in CFF.
- Omitting net borrowing from FCFE.
- Using ending rather than average assets/equity in return ratios.
- Inverting coverage ratios.
- Comparing unnormalized IFRS and US GAAP CFO.

## Revision sheet

- Analyze CFO, CFI, CFF sources/uses first; then their drivers.
- Common-size: line/total inflows or outflows; line/net revenue.
- FCFF = CFO + after-tax interest − FCInv (when CFO contains interest paid).
- FCFE = CFO − FCInv + net borrowing.
- Performance: CFO/revenue, CFO/average assets, CFO/average equity, CFO/operating income, cash flow/share.
- Coverage: CFO/debt, cash interest coverage, reinvestment, debt payment, dividend payment, investing/financing coverage.
