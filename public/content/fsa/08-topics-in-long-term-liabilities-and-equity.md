# Topics in Long-Term Liabilities and Equity

This module connects three compensation and financing arrangements—leases, pensions, and share-based awards—to recognition, measurement, presentation, disclosure, and analysis. The common analytical question is who bears the economic obligation and where that obligation appears in the statements.

## 1. Leases: substance, classification, and measurement

A lease conveys the right to control the use of an identified asset for a period in exchange for consideration. Control requires both the right to obtain substantially all economic benefits and the right to direct use. A service contract does not convey control of an identified asset.

### Worked example 1: lease or service

A customer exclusively controls a specified machine and decides what it produces for three years. The supplier cannot substitute it economically. The contract contains a lease. If the supplier can freely substitute equivalent machines and directs their operation, the arrangement is a service.

A finance lease resembles a financed purchase; an operating lease resembles a rental. Classification indicators include transfer of ownership, a reasonably certain purchase option, a lease term covering a major part of useful life, present value representing substantially all fair value, or a specialized asset with no alternative use.

### Original checklist 1: finance-lease classification

1. Ownership transfers.
2. A purchase option is reasonably certain to be exercised.
3. Lease term covers a major part of useful life.
4. Payment present value is substantially all fair value.
5. The asset has no alternative use to the lessor.

### Worked example 2: classification evidence

Payments have a present value of 186 and the asset fair value is 190. The 97.9% proportion is evidence that substantially all value is financed, supporting finance classification.

At commencement, a lessee generally recognizes a lease liability and right-of-use (ROU) asset measured from the present value of fixed payments. The liability follows the effective-interest method: interest equals beginning liability times the discount rate, principal equals payment minus interest, and closing liability equals opening liability minus principal.

### Worked example 3: initial measurement

Five year-end payments of 100,000 discounted at 10% have a present value of approximately 379,079. That amount is the initial liability and, absent other adjustments, the initial ROU asset.

### Original table 2: first-period amortization

| Item | Calculation | Amount |
|---|---|---:|
| Opening liability | Present value | 379,079 |
| Interest | 379,079 × 10% | 37,908 |
| Cash payment | Contract | 100,000 |
| Principal | 100,000 − 37,908 | 62,092 |
| Closing liability | 379,079 − 62,092 | 316,987 |

IFRS uses a single lessee model for most leases: separate interest and ROU depreciation/amortization create a front-loaded total expense. US GAAP finance-lease accounting is similar. US GAAP operating leases present a single straight-line lease expense; ROU amortization equals lease expense minus interest so the ROU asset and liability remain aligned.

### Worked example 4: model comparison

With a 100,000 annual payment and first-year interest of 37,908, US GAAP operating-lease ROU amortization is 62,092, producing total lease expense of 100,000. Finance-model straight-line ROU amortization is about 75,816, producing first-year total expense of 113,724.

### Original table 3: lessee presentation

| Feature | IFRS/US finance | US operating |
|---|---|---|
| Income statement | Interest plus amortization | Single lease expense |
| Expense pattern | Front-loaded | Generally straight-line |
| Cash flow | Principal financing; interest per framework/policy | Entire payment operating |
| EBITDA | Higher because amortization/interest excluded | Lower because lease expense is operating |

Lessors retain the asset and recognize rental income for operating leases. For finance leases, the lessor derecognizes the underlying asset, recognizes a lease receivable, and applies effective interest. IFRS and US GAAP lessor accounting is substantially aligned for Level I analysis.

### Worked example 5: lessor receivable

A beginning receivable of 300 earns 8% interest and receives 75 cash. Interest income is 24, principal collection is 51, and the ending receivable is 249.

### Original diagram 4: lease reporting map

```text
Lessee -> recognize ROU asset + liability -> finance-style or US operating presentation
Lessor -> operating: retain asset + rent/depreciation
       -> finance: derecognize asset + recognize receivable/interest
```

## 2. Pension plans

In a defined contribution plan, the employer promises contributions; the employee bears investment and longevity risk. Period expense is generally the required contribution. In a defined benefit plan, the employer promises benefits and bears actuarial and investment risk.

### Original table 5: pension risk allocation

| Plan | Defined amount | Primary risk bearer | Employer accounting focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Defined contribution | Contribution | Employee | Contribution expense/payable |
| Defined benefit | Retirement benefit | Employer | Obligation, plan assets, funded status, cost components |

Defined benefit obligation is the present value of expected future benefits earned. Plan assets are measured at fair value. Plan assets minus obligation is funded status: a positive result is a net pension asset; a negative result is a net pension liability.

### Worked example 6: funded status

Plan assets are 900 and obligation is 1,000. Funded status is −100, reported as a net pension liability.

Service cost and net interest generally affect profit or loss. Remeasurements, including actuarial gains/losses and return differences, are reported in other comprehensive income under IFRS and are not recycled. Contributions change plan assets and cash but are not themselves the same as pension expense.

### Worked example 7: assumption sensitivity

A lower discount rate increases the present value of benefit obligations, worsens funded status, and usually increases reported pension liability. Higher expected salary growth or longevity assumptions also increase the obligation.

### Original matrix 6: defined-benefit movements

| Event | Obligation | Plan assets | Funded status |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Service earned | Up | — | Worsens |
| Employer contribution | — | Up | Improves |
| Benefits paid | Down | Down | Often neutral initially |
| Asset return above expectation | — | Up | Improves |
| Discount rate falls | Up | — | Worsens |

## 3. Stock-based compensation

Equity awards align employees with shareholders but create compensation expense and potential dilution. Stock grants, restricted stock units, and options are generally measured at grant-date fair value and recognized over the service/vesting period for awards expected to vest.

### Worked example 8: restricted stock expense

Awards with grant-date fair value of 600 vest over three years. With straight-line recognition and full expected vesting, annual compensation expense is 200.

Stock options have asymmetric payoff. Intrinsic value at exercise is the excess of market price over exercise price, never below zero. Grant-date option fair value incorporates share price, exercise price, expected volatility, expected life, dividends, and the risk-free rate—often through an option-pricing model. Higher volatility generally raises option value.

### Worked example 9: option payoff

An employee exercises 1,000 options with exercise price 30 when shares trade at 44. Intrinsic value is 14,000. If market price is 26, the options are out of the money and intrinsic value is zero.

### Original table 7: award comparison

| Award | Employee payoff | Valuation feature | Analytical issue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Restricted shares/RSUs | Share value if vested | Share price, adjusted for terms | Dilution and retention |
| Options | Upside above exercise price | Option model and volatility | Convex incentives and estimation |
| Cash-settled SAR/phantom award | Cash linked to share performance | Remeasured liability | Earnings volatility |

Compensation expense is non-cash when equity-settled and is added back in indirect CFO, but it is still an economic cost to shareholders. Analysts should not ignore expense merely because cash is not paid.

## 4. Presentation, disclosures, and analysis

Long-term debt disclosures cover maturity schedules, interest rates, covenants, collateral, defaults, and current portions. Lease notes describe expense, cash paid, ROU assets/liabilities, maturity analysis, discount rates, and remaining terms. Pension notes explain obligations, plan assets, funded status, expense components, assumptions, asset allocation, and expected contributions. Share-based compensation notes describe plan terms, award activity, grant-date fair value, valuation assumptions, expense, unrecognized cost, and remaining recognition period.

### Worked example 10: current classification

Of a 500 bond, 60 is due within twelve months. The 60 is a current liability and 440 remains non-current, absent refinancing or covenant facts changing classification.

### Original checklist 8: integrated disclosure audit

- Reconcile lease liabilities and inspect maturity concentration.
- Compare operating versus finance presentation before comparing EBITDA and CFO.
- Recalculate pension funded status and stress discount-rate assumptions.
- Separate pension contribution cash flows from accounting expense.
- Quantify unrecognized stock-compensation cost and remaining vesting period.
- Evaluate dilution, option assumptions, debt covenants, and refinancing needs.

### Worked example 11: lease-adjusted analysis

Recognizing a previously omitted lease adds both assets and debt. Leverage normally rises, asset turnover usually falls, and current ratio may fall because a current liability is recognized. EBITDA may increase under finance-style presentation even though economics do not improve.

### Worked example 12: unrecognized compensation

Unrecognized compensation cost is 630 with a weighted-average remaining period of 3.2 years. A rough straight-line estimate is about 197 per full year, with the residual recognized in the partial final year; actual recognition follows vesting schedules and forfeiture estimates.

## Formula recap

1. Lease liability/ROU asset at commencement = present value of fixed lease payments.
2. Lessee interest expense = beginning lease liability × discount rate.
3. Principal repayment = lease payment − interest expense.
4. Closing lease liability = opening liability + interest − payment.
5. Straight-line ROU amortization = initial ROU asset / lease term.
6. US operating-lease ROU amortization = single lease expense − interest.
7. Lessor interest income = beginning lease receivable × implicit rate; principal collected = receipt − interest.
8. Funded status = fair value of plan assets − defined benefit obligation.
9. Defined contribution expense = contribution attributable to the period, adjusted for accrual/prepayment.
10. Stock-compensation expense = grant-date fair value expected to vest / service period under straight-line allocation.
11. Option intrinsic value = max(0, market price − exercise price) × options exercised.

The core analytical discipline is to preserve economic substance across presentation choices: identify the obligation, reconstruct its measurement and timing, and then normalize ratios and cash-flow classifications before comparing issuers.
