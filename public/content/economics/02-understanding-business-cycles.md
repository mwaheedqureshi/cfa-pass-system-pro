# Understanding Business Cycles

## Chapter overview

Economies do not grow along a straight line. Output, employment, spending, and credit all move in recurring waves that are rarely the same length and never perfectly predictable. This chapter gives you a disciplined vocabulary for that motion: the four phases of the business cycle, the separate but related rhythm of the credit cycle, and the leading, coincident, and lagging indicators analysts use to work out where an economy currently stands and where it is heading next.

## Why it matters

Every top-down investment decision — sector rotation, credit-quality screening, duration positioning, currency exposure — starts with a view on the cycle. Analysts who can read output-gap dynamics, anticipate a credit-cycle turn, and correctly classify an indicator's timing produce more defensible calls than analysts who simply react to the latest headline data release.

## Official 2027 learning outcomes

The curriculum metadata above contains the authorized outcomes. In practical terms, be ready to:

- describe the business cycle and its phases;
- describe credit cycles; and
- describe how resource use, consumer and business activity, housing sector activity, and external trade sector activity vary over the business cycle, and describe their measurement using economic indicators.

## Prerequisites

You should be comfortable reading a line chart and comparing a growth rate with a trend. No prior economics lesson is required, though familiarity with basic supply-and-demand reasoning from [The Firm and Market Structures](/lessons/economics-firm-market-01) is useful background.

## 1. Three ways to define a cycle

Economists do not describe "the business cycle" with a single measure. Three related but distinct cycle concepts appear side by side in practice, and mixing them up is a common source of confusion.

| Cycle concept | What fluctuates | Practical note |
|---|---|---|
| Classical cycle | The **level** of aggregate economic activity (for example, real GDP itself rising and falling) | Rarely used today because modern economies grow most of the time; an outright decline in the level of GDP is a comparatively rare, severe event |
| Growth cycle | **Deviations of actual output from its long-term trend (potential) output** — the output gap | The definition used throughout this reading and the one most useful for classifying the four phases below |
| Growth rate cycle | The **growth rate** of GDP itself (accelerating or decelerating), rather than the level or the gap | Turning points in the growth rate are usually recognized earliest of the three, because a deceleration in growth shows up before the growth rate turns negative or before a gap actually closes |

### Worked example 1 — Distinguishing the three cycle concepts

**Problem.** Real GDP growth slows from 4% to 1% while the level of GDP keeps rising and stays above trend. Which cycle concept turns first, and has a classical-cycle recession occurred?

**Method.** Compare what each concept tracks: level, gap, or growth rate.

**Step-by-step solution.** GDP is still rising (no classical-cycle downturn) and still above trend (positive growth-cycle gap persists), but its growth rate has already decelerated sharply.

**Final answer.** The growth rate cycle turns first; no classical-cycle recession has occurred.

**Interpretation.** A slowing growth rate is an early warning sign long before either the level or the trend-relative gap actually turns negative.

**Exam insight.** "The economy is slowing" and "the economy is shrinking" describe different cycle concepts — read the question carefully for which one is being tested.

> **Knowledge check 1.** Which cycle concept is least commonly used in modern practice? **Answer:** The classical cycle, because sustained declines in the level of GDP are comparatively rare.

## 2. The four phases of the business cycle

Using the growth-cycle definition, the business cycle moves through four named phases, separated by two turning points — the **peak** and the **trough** — rather than treating the turning points themselves as phases.

| Phase | Output gap | Typical activity | Employment | Inflation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Recovery | Negative gap, narrowing (starts at the trough) | Below potential, but improving | Layoffs slow; employers rely on overtime and temporary staff before net hiring resumes; unemployment stays elevated | Moderate |
| Expansion | Positive gap opening (output moves above potential); a late, overheated stage is informally called a **boom** | Above-trend growth; consumer spending and business investment both accelerate | Hiring broadens beyond overtime/temp staff; unemployment falls | Picks up modestly |
| Slowdown | Positive gap at its widest, then narrowing (ends at the peak) | Still above trend, but decelerating | Hiring continues but at a decreasing pace; unemployment still falling, but more slowly | Accelerates further |
| Contraction | Negative gap opening | Below trend; confidence and spending decline; if the decline is unusually severe, it may be called a **depression** | Overtime and hiring are cut first, then outright layoffs follow; unemployment rises | Decelerates, typically with a lag |

### Decision flow — reading a phase from the output gap

```text
      Is output currently above potential (positive gap)?
                        │
              ┌─────────┴─────────┐
             YES                  NO
              │                    │
     Is the positive gap    Is the negative gap
     still widening?        still widening?
              │                    │
      ┌───────┴───────┐    ┌───────┴───────┐
     YES              NO  YES              NO
      │                │    │                │
  EXPANSION       SLOWDOWN  CONTRACTION   RECOVERY
  (gap opening,    (gap at   (gap opening,  (gap narrowing,
   moving toward    its       moving toward   moving toward
   the peak)        widest)   the trough)     the trough exit)
```

### Worked example 2 — Identifying a phase from a described output gap

**Problem.** An economy's output has been above potential for two years. This quarter, the gap is still positive but narrower than last quarter's reading. Which phase is the economy in?

**Method.** A positive gap that is shrinking, rather than still widening, places the economy at or past the point where growth has begun to decelerate.

**Step-by-step solution.** A positive-but-narrowing gap matches the definition of Slowdown, which runs from the widest point of the positive gap up to the peak.

**Final answer.** Slowdown.

**Interpretation.** The economy has not yet contracted — output is still above potential — but the pace of above-trend growth is fading.

**Exam insight.** Do not equate "still growing" with "still expanding" in the phase sense; check whether the gap is widening or narrowing.

### Worked example 3 — Calculating an output gap

**Problem.** Potential (trend) GDP is estimated at 21.4 trillion in local currency, and actual GDP is 21.0 trillion. Calculate the output gap in currency terms and as a percentage of potential.

**Method.** Apply the output-gap formula.

**Step-by-step solution.** Output gap = 21.0 − 21.4 = −0.4 trillion. As a percentage of potential: (−0.4 / 21.4) × 100 ≈ −1.9%.

**Final answer.** The output gap is −0.4 trillion, or approximately −1.9% of potential GDP.

**Interpretation.** A negative gap of this size indicates the economy is operating below its productive capacity, consistent with Contraction or early Recovery depending on the trend of recent readings.

**Exam insight.** A negative sign alone does not distinguish Contraction from Recovery — you also need to know whether the gap is widening (Contraction) or narrowing (Recovery).

> **Knowledge check 2.** During which phase does the output gap reach its most positive (widest above-trend) reading? **Answer:** At the boundary between Expansion and Slowdown — the peak.

## 3. Turning points and dating a cycle

The peak and trough are turning points between phases, not phases in their own right. Identifying them in real time is harder than the clean phase table suggests.

A frequently cited shortcut — "a recession is two consecutive quarters of negative real GDP growth" — is explicitly too simplistic for professional use. It ignores employment, income, industrial production, and sales, and it can misdate short but severe downturns or miss slow-motion ones. Official dating committees (for example, the U.S. National Bureau of Economic Research's Business Cycle Dating Committee) instead review a broad basket of monthly and quarterly variables, and their conclusions are typically announced well after the turning point has already passed, and are subject to later revision.

### Worked example 4 — Why the two-quarter rule can mislead

**Problem.** An economy's real GDP falls in Q1, rises slightly in Q2, and falls sharply in Q3 and Q4. Employment, income, and sales all decline steadily from Q1 through Q4. Does the two-consecutive-quarter rule correctly flag this downturn?

**Method.** Apply the rule mechanically, then compare it with the broader evidence.

**Step-by-step solution.** The rule looks for two consecutive negative quarters; the single positive quarter (Q2) breaks that pattern, so a mechanical application would not flag a continuous recession even though four independent indicators point to one throughout the year.

**Final answer.** No — the simple rule can fail to flag a recession that a broader indicator review would identify.

**Interpretation.** This is exactly why dating committees look at multiple broad measures rather than a single GDP-growth rule.

**Exam insight.** Expect the exam to reward recognizing the two-quarter rule's limitation, not just reciting it.

### Worked example 5 — Investor behavior across phases

**Problem.** An allocator observes that government-bond demand and defensive-sector allocations are both rising while equity risk appetite is falling. Which phase is most consistent with this behavior, and what typically happens to equity markets just before the next trough?

**Method.** Match the described flight-to-safety behavior to a phase, then apply the empirical lead time of equities relative to the real economy.

**Step-by-step solution.** Rising demand for safe assets and falling risk appetite are most consistent with Contraction. Equity markets are typically forward-looking and have historically begun to recover roughly three to six months before the real economy reaches its trough.

**Final answer.** Contraction; equities tend to lead the trough by several months.

**Interpretation.** Waiting for official confirmation that a recession has ended means missing the early part of a typical equity recovery.

**Exam insight.** "Markets lead the cycle" is a recurring theme — expect it to be tested through indicator classification as well as narrative questions.

## 4. Credit cycles

A credit cycle describes the **changing availability and pricing of credit** — chiefly private-sector lending for business investment and household real-estate purchases — rather than output itself. Lenders extend credit more freely and cheaply when the economy is strong, and tighten it — making credit scarcer and more expensive — when the economy weakens. Because tighter credit can itself depress spending and asset prices, this behavior is pro-cyclical and can amplify the swings it responds to.

Credit cycles are related to business cycles but are **not synchronized with them**, and empirically they run **longer, deeper, and sharper**. Two consequences matter most for analysts:

- A recession that coincides with financial disruption (falling house or equity prices, credit contraction) tends to be **longer and deeper** than one without it.
- A recovery accompanied by rapid credit and house-price growth tends to be **stronger** than one without it.
- Strong peaks in the credit cycle are closely associated with subsequent systemic banking crises, which is why policymakers increasingly use targeted **macroprudential policy** — rather than only broad monetary or fiscal policy — to address credit-cycle excess specifically.

| Dimension | Business cycle | Credit cycle |
|---|---|---|
| Core variable tracked | Output relative to potential (the growth-cycle gap) | Availability and pricing of credit; often proxied with credit, the credit-to-GDP ratio, and real house prices |
| Typical duration relative to the other | Shorter | Longer |
| Typical amplitude relative to the other | Shallower | Deeper and sharper |
| Synchronization | Related to, but not perfectly aligned with, the credit cycle | Related to, but not perfectly aligned with, the business cycle |
| Distinctive risk signal | Recession dating (peak/trough) | A strong cyclical peak often precedes a systemic banking crisis |

### Worked example 6 — Why a credit-driven recession is deeper

**Problem.** Two economies enter recession at the same time. Economy A's downturn coincides with a sharp fall in house prices and a contraction in bank lending; Economy B's downturn shows no comparable financial disruption. Which economy's recession is more likely to be longer and deeper, and why?

**Method.** Apply the empirical relationship between financial-cycle disruption and recession severity.

**Step-by-step solution.** Economy A's recession coincides with a credit-cycle contraction (falling house prices, tighter lending), which the reading associates with longer, deeper downturns; Economy B's recession lacks that reinforcing mechanism.

**Final answer.** Economy A.

**Interpretation.** The credit channel can turn an ordinary cyclical slowdown into a more severe and persistent downturn.

**Exam insight.** Look for house-price and lending-standard language as the tell that a question is really about the credit cycle, not just the business cycle.

### Worked example 7 — Calculating a credit-to-GDP ratio

**Problem.** Outstanding private-sector credit is 148 billion and nominal GDP is 200 billion. One year later, credit has grown to 168 billion while nominal GDP has grown to 206 billion. Calculate the credit-to-GDP ratio in each year and interpret the change.

**Method.** Apply the credit-to-GDP ratio formula in both years and compare.

**Step-by-step solution.** Year 1: 148 / 200 = 0.74, or 74%. Year 2: 168 / 206 ≈ 0.816, or about 81.6%. The ratio rose by roughly 7.6 percentage points.

**Final answer.** The credit-to-GDP ratio rose from about 74% to about 81.6%.

**Interpretation.** Credit is growing faster than the economy that has to service it — a rising ratio like this is one of the signals used to gauge the intensity of a credit-cycle expansion.

**Exam insight.** A rising credit-to-GDP ratio is a symptom to interpret in context, not proof by itself that a crisis is imminent.

> **Knowledge check 3.** Are credit cycles perfectly synchronized with business cycles? **Answer:** No — they are related but typically longer, deeper, and sharper, and their timing does not exactly match the business cycle's.

## 5. Resource use over the cycle: labor, capital spending, and inventories

The reading operationalizes "resource use" through three concrete channels: the workforce, capital spending, and inventories.

**Workforce and company costs.** Employers try to retain trained staff through a downturn because hiring and training are costly, so they typically cut overtime and temporary staff first and rely on lean staffing near the trough before turning to layoffs. As a result, **employment levels tend to lag the cycle**: unemployment keeps rising for a period after output has already begun to recover, and keeps falling for a period after output has already begun to slow.

**Capital spending.** Business capital expenditure is one of the most procyclical and volatile components of GDP. Because new orders precede shipments and installation, **new orders for capital goods are a widely watched leading signal**. Short-lead equipment (technology, software) tends to be ordered and cancelled quickly; long-lead equipment (heavy construction, large facilities) responds more slowly and lags.

**Inventories.** The **inventory-to-sales (I/S) ratio** is the key metric. It typically falls during Recovery (sales growth outpaces production), stays broadly stable during Expansion, rises during Slowdown (a warning sign that production has outrun sales), and falls back during Contraction as production is cut faster than sales.

### Worked example 8 — Employment as a lagging response

**Problem.** Output growth reaccelerated four months ago, but the unemployment rate has only just stopped rising. Is this pattern consistent with the reading's description of the labor market over the cycle?

**Method.** Compare the observed timing with the stated lag between output and employment.

**Step-by-step solution.** Employers avoid layoffs during the downturn and rely on overtime/temporary staffing rather than immediate hiring once output turns up, so measured unemployment continuing to rise (or only just stabilizing) after output has already recovered is the expected lagged pattern.

**Final answer.** Yes, this is consistent with employment lagging the cycle.

**Interpretation.** A still-rising unemployment rate does not necessarily mean the recovery has stalled.

**Exam insight.** Treat "employment lags the cycle" as a default assumption unless a question specifies otherwise.

### Worked example 9 — Capital spending as a leading signal

**Problem.** New orders for non-defense capital goods rise sharply for three consecutive months while current industrial production is still flat. What does this most likely foreshadow?

**Method.** Apply the procyclical, order-leads-shipment logic of capital spending.

**Step-by-step solution.** Because new orders precede shipments and installation, a sustained rise in new orders typically foreshadows a pickup in production and shipments in coming months, even though current production has not yet moved.

**Final answer.** Rising industrial production and capital spending are likely to follow.

**Interpretation.** New orders are a forward-looking signal precisely because of the lead time between ordering equipment and installing it.

**Exam insight.** Distinguish "new orders" (leading) from "shipments" or "production" (closer to coincident) when a question tests indicator timing.

### Worked example 10 — Reading the inventory-to-sales ratio

**Problem.** Inventory is 84 million and sales are 70 million this quarter, versus inventory of 80 million and sales of 76 million last quarter. Calculate the I/S ratio in both quarters and identify the most likely phase transition.

**Method.** Apply the I/S ratio formula and compare the trend.

**Step-by-step solution.** This quarter: 84 / 70 = 1.20. Last quarter: 80 / 76 ≈ 1.05. The ratio has risen from about 1.05 to 1.20.

**Final answer.** The inventory-to-sales ratio rose from about 1.05 to 1.20.

**Interpretation.** A rising I/S ratio signals that production has outrun sales — the pattern the reading associates with Slowdown, as goods pile up faster than they can be sold.

**Exam insight.** A rising ratio is a warning sign about the future pace of production, not proof that a contraction has already started.

> **Knowledge check 4.** Which resource-use channel is described as one of the most procyclical and volatile components of GDP? **Answer:** Capital spending (business investment).

## 6. Economic indicators: leading, coincident, and lagging

An **economic indicator** is a single data series used to infer the state of the economy. Indicators are classified by the timing of their turning points relative to the overall economy:

- **Leading indicators** turn *before* the economy and are used to anticipate the near-term future state.
- **Coincident indicators** turn *at roughly the same time* as the economy and describe its present state.
- **Lagging indicators** turn *after* the economy and are used to confirm a phase that has already begun.
- **Composite indicators** combine several individual series that tend to move together, rather than relying on one series alone.

| Category | Example series | What it captures |
|---|---|---|
| Leading | Average weekly manufacturing hours; initial unemployment claims; new orders for consumer goods; new orders for non-defense capital goods (excluding aircraft); building permits; a broad stock index; a leading credit index; the long-term-minus-short-term interest-rate spread; consumer expectations | Signals that typically move before broad economic activity turns |
| Coincident | Industrial production; real personal income; manufacturing and trade sales; nonfarm payroll employment | Signals that move roughly together with current activity |
| Lagging | Average duration of unemployment; the inventory-to-sales ratio; unit labor costs; inflation; the prime lending rate; the ratio of consumer installment debt to income; commercial and industrial loans outstanding | Signals that confirm a phase only after it is well underway |

### Decision flow — using the three indicator types together

```text
     New reading arrives — which type of indicator is it?
                        │
        ┌───────────────┼────────────────┐
     LEADING         COINCIDENT        LAGGING
        │                │                │
  Use to form a     Use to confirm    Use to confirm
  near-term view     the economy's     that a phase has
  of what may        present state     already been
  happen next                          underway for a while
        │                │                │
        └────────────────┴────────────────┘
                 Combine all three before
                 drawing a phase conclusion
```

### Worked example 11 — Classifying indicators by timing

**Problem.** Classify each of the following as leading, coincident, or lagging: (a) building permits, (b) nonfarm payroll employment, (c) the average duration of unemployment.

**Method.** Match each series to its official classification.

**Step-by-step solution.** (a) Building permits are issued before construction activity occurs, so they are leading. (b) Nonfarm payrolls move together with current activity, so they are coincident. (c) The average duration of unemployment continues to rise after a recovery has begun, so it is lagging.

**Final answer.** (a) Leading, (b) Coincident, (c) Lagging.

**Interpretation.** Timing, not the subject matter, determines an indicator's classification.

**Exam insight.** Do not classify by "sounds forward-looking" alone — rely on the documented empirical timing of each named series.

### Worked example 12 — Calculating a diffusion index

**Problem.** A composite indicator has five components. This month, three components rose by more than 0.05%, one was roughly flat (a change smaller than 0.05% in either direction), and one fell by more than 0.05%. Calculate the diffusion index.

**Method.** Score each component 1.0 if rising, 0.5 if flat, 0 if falling; sum the scores, divide by the number of components, and multiply by 100.

**Step-by-step solution.** Score = (1.0 + 1.0 + 1.0 + 0.5 + 0) = 3.5. Diffusion index = (3.5 / 5) × 100 = 70.

**Final answer.** The diffusion index is 70.

**Interpretation.** A reading of 70 means a clear majority of the components are moving in the same (rising) direction as the composite — the breadth of the move is broad, even before considering its magnitude.

**Exam insight.** The diffusion index measures how many components are moving together, not how large the move is; a narrow move driven by one large component can still produce a low diffusion index.

### Worked example 13 — Interpreting a change in the diffusion index

**Problem.** Last month's diffusion index for the same five-component composite was 50. This month's index is 70, as calculated above. What does the change most likely indicate?

**Method.** Compare breadth, not magnitude, between the two readings.

**Step-by-step solution.** The percentage of components moving in the same direction as the overall index rose from 50% to 70%, meaning the underlying improvement broadened across components rather than being carried by only one or two of them.

**Final answer.** The recent move has become broader-based.

**Interpretation.** A broadening diffusion index tends to be read as a more convincing signal than a narrow move by a single dominant component.

**Exam insight.** Two composite indexes can show the identical percentage change while having very different diffusion indexes — the diffusion index adds information the headline number alone does not provide.

> **Knowledge check 5.** Which indicator category is used to confirm that a phase has already been underway for some time? **Answer:** Lagging indicators.

## 7. Housing and external trade activity over the cycle

The official reading gives housing and external-trade activity comparatively brief, indicator-level treatment rather than a dedicated framework, so this section keeps to general, original application built on ordinary open-economy logic rather than asserting official-source-backed detail beyond what the curriculum states.

**Housing.** Housing activity is captured in the indicator taxonomy mainly through **building permits** (a leading indicator, because a permit precedes construction) and house prices, which also feed into the credit-cycle proxy discussed in Section 4. Housing is often described as one of the more interest-rate-sensitive sectors of the economy, since new construction and home purchases are typically financed with borrowed money, making housing activity an early transmitter of tightening or loosening credit conditions into the broader economy.

**External trade.** As a general, open-economy relationship, a trading partner's own growth tends to raise its demand for a country's exports, while an increase in domestic income tends to raise domestic demand for imports. External trade activity is therefore linked to both the domestic cycle (through import demand) and to trading partners' cycles (through export demand), which is one reason business cycles across closely connected economies tend to move together rather than in isolation.

### Worked example 14 — Housing as an interest-rate-sensitive, credit-linked sector

**Problem.** A central bank sharply raises interest rates to slow an overheating economy. Building permits fall for several consecutive months afterward. Is this consistent with housing's role as described above, and would you expect this signal to appear before or after broad output growth actually slows?

**Method.** Apply housing's rate sensitivity and building permits' leading classification.

**Step-by-step solution.** Higher borrowing costs discourage new construction and home purchases, consistent with housing's rate sensitivity; because building permits are a leading indicator, the drop in permits would be expected to appear before broad output growth visibly slows.

**Final answer.** Yes, this is consistent; the signal should appear before broad output growth slows.

**Interpretation.** Housing can act as an early transmission channel through which tighter credit conditions reach the wider economy.

**Exam insight.** Building permits combine two ideas tested separately elsewhere in this module — leading-indicator timing and credit-cycle transmission.

### Worked example 15 — Export demand and a trading partner's cycle

**Problem.** Country A's largest trading partner, Country B, enters a strong expansion. All else equal, what is the most likely effect on Country A's exports, and why?

**Method.** Apply the open-economy relationship between a trading partner's growth and export demand.

**Step-by-step solution.** As Country B's income rises during its expansion, its demand for goods — including goods imported from Country A — tends to rise as well, all else equal.

**Final answer.** Country A's exports are likely to increase.

**Interpretation.** This is one channel through which business cycles can transmit across closely linked economies.

**Exam insight.** Keep the direction of the relationship straight: a trading partner's growth raises demand for your exports; your own growth raises your demand for imports.

## 8. Putting the indicators together: an analyst workflow

1. Identify which phase the growth-cycle output gap most likely describes, using the table in Section 2.
2. Check leading indicators for signals about the near-term direction.
3. Confirm the present state with coincident indicators.
4. Use lagging indicators only to confirm — never to predict — a phase that other evidence already suggests has begun.
5. Calculate a diffusion index when several components of a composite indicator are available, to judge how broad-based a move is.
6. Cross-check credit-cycle signals (credit growth, the credit-to-GDP ratio, house prices) separately, since they can diverge in timing and severity from the business cycle itself.
7. State the phase conclusion as a probability-weighted judgment, not a certainty — official dating committees confirm turning points only well after the fact.

### Worked example 16 — Integrating leading, coincident, and lagging signals

**Problem.** This month: initial unemployment claims are falling (leading), industrial production is rising (coincident), and the average duration of unemployment is still rising (lagging). What phase does this combination most likely describe?

**Method.** Apply the workflow above: leading and coincident signals both point toward improvement; the lagging indicator is expected to keep confirming the *prior* weak phase for a while even after a turn has begun.

**Step-by-step solution.** Falling initial claims (leading) and rising industrial production (coincident) both indicate that activity has already turned up. The still-rising average duration of unemployment is fully consistent with a lagging indicator that has not yet caught up to the turn.

**Final answer.** The economy has most likely entered Recovery, even though the lagging labor-market indicator has not yet confirmed it.

**Interpretation.** This is exactly the sequencing the leading/coincident/lagging framework predicts — a real-time analyst should not wait for lagging confirmation before updating a view.

**Exam insight.** Questions that mix indicator types are testing whether you can sequence them correctly, not just classify each one in isolation.

## Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Business cycle | Recurrent but not periodic fluctuations in economic activity, typically lasting more than a year and affecting many sectors at once. |
| Classical cycle | Fluctuations in the level of aggregate economic activity. |
| Growth cycle | Fluctuations of actual output around its long-term trend (potential) output; the output gap. |
| Growth rate cycle | Fluctuations in the growth rate of GDP; turning points are usually recognized earliest of the three cycle concepts. |
| Output gap | The difference between actual and potential (trend) output. |
| Recovery | The phase from the trough in which the negative output gap narrows. |
| Expansion | The phase in which the positive output gap opens, potentially reaching a late-stage "boom." |
| Slowdown | The phase in which the positive output gap is at its widest and then narrows toward the peak. |
| Contraction | The phase in which the negative output gap opens after the peak. |
| Peak | The turning point between Slowdown and Contraction. |
| Trough | The turning point between Contraction and Recovery. |
| Credit cycle | Fluctuations in the availability and pricing of credit, generally longer, deeper, and sharper than the business cycle and not fully synchronized with it. |
| Macroprudential policy | Policy aimed specifically at containing credit- or financial-cycle excess, distinct from broad monetary or fiscal policy. |
| Inventory-to-sales (I/S) ratio | Inventory divided by sales; a lagging indicator that rises when production outruns sales. |
| Leading indicator | A series whose turning points typically precede the economy's turning points. |
| Coincident indicator | A series whose turning points typically occur at roughly the same time as the economy's. |
| Lagging indicator | A series whose turning points typically occur after the economy's, useful for confirmation. |
| Composite indicator | An index built from multiple individual indicators that tend to move together. |
| Diffusion index | The percentage of a composite indicator's components moving in the same direction as the overall index; a measure of breadth, not magnitude. |
| Credit-to-GDP ratio | Outstanding private-sector credit divided by nominal GDP; a common component of composite financial-cycle measures. |

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Treating the peak and trough as phases rather than as turning points between phases.
- Applying the "two consecutive quarters of negative growth" rule as if it were the official, complete definition of a recession.
- Assuming employment turns at the same time as output rather than lagging it.
- Confusing a leading indicator with a coincident or lagging one because of what the series "sounds like" rather than its documented timing.
- Reading the diffusion index as a measure of the size of a move rather than its breadth.
- Assuming the credit cycle and business cycle always move in lockstep.
- Forgetting that a rising inventory-to-sales ratio is a warning sign about future production, not proof that a contraction has already begun.
- Mixing up the direction of the export/import relationship in open-economy reasoning.

## Memory aids

- **Recovery, Expansion, Slowdown, Contraction — RESC** turns the four phases into one pronounceable cue in their correct order.
- **Gap governs the phase:** a widening gap means Expansion or Contraction; a narrowing gap means Slowdown or Recovery.
- **Leads predict, coincidents describe, lags confirm.**
- **Credit runs longer, deeper, sharper** than the business cycle it interacts with.
- **Diffusion is about how many, not how much.**

## Exam tips

- When a question describes an output-gap trajectory, sketch (mentally or on scratch paper) whether the gap is positive/negative and widening/narrowing before naming the phase.
- Watch for questions that supply a mix of leading, coincident, and lagging signals and ask you to sequence — not merely classify — them.
- When credit-cycle language appears (house prices, lending standards, credit growth), keep it analytically separate from plain output-based business-cycle reasoning.
- For diffusion-index questions, recompute the score per component before selecting an answer; do not estimate.
- For housing and external-trade questions, rely on the general relationships taught here (rate sensitivity, income-driven trade demand) rather than assuming detailed official coverage beyond what this lesson states.

| Housing | Rate-sensitive; building permits are a leading indicator |
| External trade | Partner growth raises your exports; your own growth raises your imports |

## 30-second summary

The business cycle moves through Recovery, Expansion, Slowdown, and Contraction, defined by whether the output gap is positive or negative and widening or narrowing, with the peak and trough as the turning points between phases. The credit cycle tracks the availability and pricing of credit; it is related to the business cycle but runs longer, deeper, and sharper, and a strong credit-cycle peak often precedes a banking crisis. Employment lags the cycle, capital spending is highly procyclical with new orders leading, and the inventory-to-sales ratio rises in Slowdown. Leading indicators predict, coincident indicators describe, and lagging indicators confirm; a diffusion index measures how broad-based a composite indicator's move is, not its size. Housing is rate-sensitive and captured mainly through building permits, while external trade activity links a country's cycle to its trading partners'.
