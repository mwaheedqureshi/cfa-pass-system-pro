# The Firm and Market Structures

## Chapter overview

A firm creates value only when the revenue from its output justifies the economic cost of the resources it uses. This chapter builds a decision framework from that simple idea. You will locate breakeven and shutdown points, distinguish economies from diseconomies of scale, compare the four principal market structures, analyze pricing in monopolistic competition and oligopoly, and judge what concentration statistics can—and cannot—reveal.

## Why it matters

Market structure shapes margins, reinvestment opportunities, competitive threats, and the durability of profits. Analysts therefore need more than industry labels. They must connect demand, marginal revenue, marginal cost, entry barriers, and strategic interaction to a defensible forecast.

## Official 2027 learning outcomes

The curriculum metadata above contains the authorized outcomes. In practical terms, be ready to:

- determine and interpret breakeven and shutdown points and the cost effects of scale under competitive and imperfectly competitive conditions;
- describe perfect competition, monopolistic competition, oligopoly, and pure monopoly;
- analyze supply, demand, optimal output, and pricing in monopolistic competition;
- analyze supply, demand, optimal output, and pricing in oligopoly; and
- identify market structure and explain the uses and limitations of concentration measures.

## Prerequisites

You should be comfortable with slopes, averages, and the distinction between a stock and a flow. No prior economics lesson is required. The most useful earlier quantitative skill is careful interpretation: a calculated threshold is a decision boundary, not a forecast.

## 1. The economic decision frame

Economic profit subtracts every opportunity cost, including the return owners could earn in the next-best use of their capital and labor. Accounting profit generally subtracts explicit recorded expenses. A firm can therefore report positive accounting profit while earning zero or negative economic profit.

| Measure | Core question | Treatment of opportunity cost | Analyst use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accounting profit | Did recorded revenue exceed recorded expense? | Usually excludes implicit owner costs | Reporting and reconciliation |
| Economic profit | Did the firm outperform the next-best use of all resources? | Includes explicit and implicit costs | Entry, exit, and value creation |
| Normal profit | Is the required opportunity-cost return covered? | Exactly covered | Long-run competitive equilibrium |

Total revenue is price times quantity: `TR = P × Q`. Total cost is fixed cost plus variable cost: `TC = FC + VC`. Economic profit is `π = TR − TC`. Average total cost is `ATC = TC / Q`; average variable cost is `AVC = VC / Q`. Marginal revenue and marginal cost measure the change caused by one more unit.

The general output rule is to expand while the incremental benefit exceeds the incremental cost. An interior profit maximum occurs where `MR = MC`, provided marginal cost is rising through marginal revenue. The equality alone is not enough if it identifies a minimum or another non-maximizing point.

### Worked example 1 — Economic versus accounting profit

**Problem.** A founder records revenue of €520,000 and explicit costs of €430,000. Her invested capital could earn €35,000 elsewhere, and comparable employment would pay her €70,000. Find accounting and economic profit.

**Method.** Subtract recorded costs for accounting profit, then subtract all opportunity costs for economic profit.

**Step-by-step solution.** Accounting profit is `€520,000 − €430,000 = €90,000`. Implicit costs are `€35,000 + €70,000 = €105,000`. Economic profit is `€90,000 − €105,000 = −€15,000`.

**Final answer.** Accounting profit is €90,000; economic profit is −€15,000.

**Interpretation.** The business is profitable in the accounting sense but underperforms the founder's next-best alternatives by €15,000.

**Exam insight.** Never treat an implicit cost as irrelevant merely because no cash payment appears.

> **Knowledge check 1.** A firm earns exactly zero economic profit. Has it failed? **Answer:** No. It has covered explicit costs and the required opportunity-cost return.

### Worked example 2 — Selecting output with marginal analysis

**Problem.** A firm can produce a fifth unit for marginal cost €24 and sell it for marginal revenue €31. A sixth unit has MC €35 and MR €29. What output is preferred?

**Method.** Produce an incremental unit when MR exceeds MC; stop before the next unit destroys profit.

**Step-by-step solution.** Unit five adds `€31 − €24 = €7` to profit. Unit six subtracts `€35 − €29 = €6` from profit. Produce through unit five, not unit six.

**Final answer.** Five units.

**Interpretation.** The optimum lies where the sign of incremental profit changes.

**Exam insight.** “MR = MC” is often implemented with discrete data by finding the last unit for which MR is at least MC.

## 2. Breakeven and shutdown are different thresholds

Breakeven asks whether revenue covers total economic cost. At a given output, it occurs when `TR = TC`, equivalently `P = ATC` for a price-taking firm. The breakeven quantity under linear unit contribution is `Q_BE = FC / (P − V)`, where `V` is variable cost per unit and `P − V` must be positive.

Shutdown is a short-run operating decision. Fixed cost may remain payable whether production continues or stops. Operating contributes toward fixed cost whenever revenue covers variable cost. A price-taking firm shuts down when price falls below minimum AVC; it is indifferent at the minimum AVC.

| Price relationship | Short-run action | Economic profit |
|---|---|---|
| `P > ATC` | Operate | Positive |
| `P = ATC` | Operate; breakeven | Zero |
| `AVC < P < ATC` | Operate in short run | Negative, but smaller than fixed cost |
| `P = min AVC` | Indifferent at shutdown point | Negative by fixed cost |
| `P < min AVC` | Shut down in short run | Lose fixed cost only |

### Worked example 3 — Breakeven quantity

**Problem.** A service has fixed cost €180,000, price €75 per subscription, and variable cost €45 per subscription. Find breakeven volume.

**Method.** Divide fixed cost by unit contribution.

**Step-by-step solution.** Contribution is `€75 − €45 = €30`. Thus `Q_BE = €180,000 / €30 = 6,000` subscriptions.

**Final answer.** 6,000 subscriptions.

**Interpretation.** Each subscription contributes €30 toward fixed cost; 6,000 contributions exactly cover it.

**Exam insight.** If price is no greater than variable cost, the linear formula has no economically meaningful positive breakeven quantity.

### Worked example 4 — Operate despite a loss

**Problem.** At the profit-maximizing output of 10,000 units, price is €18, ATC is €22, and AVC is €13. Should the firm operate in the short run, and what is its loss?

**Method.** Compare price with AVC for shutdown and with ATC for profit.

**Step-by-step solution.** Price exceeds AVC by €5, so operations contribute `€5 × 10,000 = €50,000` toward fixed cost. Loss per unit is `€22 − €18 = €4`; total loss is €40,000.

**Final answer.** Operate; short-run economic loss is €40,000.

**Interpretation.** Shutdown would forgo the €50,000 contribution and create a larger loss equal to total fixed cost.

**Exam insight.** A loss does not automatically imply shutdown.

> **Knowledge check 2.** At the shutdown point, does the firm cover fixed cost? **Answer:** No. It covers variable cost exactly; fixed cost remains uncovered.

### Worked example 5 — Shutdown decision

**Problem.** A price-taking plant faces price €9. At its best positive output, AVC is €11 and ATC is €17. Fixed cost is unavoidable this quarter. Operate or shut down?

**Method.** Compare price with the lowest attainable AVC.

**Step-by-step solution.** Each produced unit fails to recover €2 of variable cost. Production would add to the unavoidable fixed-cost loss. Shutting down avoids variable cost.

**Final answer.** Shut down in the short run.

**Interpretation.** The firm minimizes its loss by producing zero and bearing fixed cost only.

**Exam insight.** ATC is irrelevant to the immediate shutdown test once fixed costs are unavoidable.

## 3. Scale and the planning horizon

In the short run, at least one input is fixed. In the long run, the firm can change all inputs and choose plant scale. Economies of scale exist when long-run average total cost falls as output expands. Constant returns to scale produce a flat range. Diseconomies of scale occur when long-run average cost rises.

Sources of economies include specialization, spreading indivisible systems over more units, and purchasing power. Sources of diseconomies include coordination delays, bureaucracy, communication failures, and input bottlenecks. Minimum efficient scale is the smallest output at which long-run average cost reaches its minimum.

| Cost pattern as output grows | Classification | Possible cause | Competitive implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| LRATC falls | Economies of scale | specialization or fixed-system spreading | favors larger efficient producers |
| LRATC flat | Constant returns | inputs scale proportionally | size alone gives little cost advantage |
| LRATC rises | Diseconomies of scale | coordination complexity | limits efficient firm size |

### Worked example 6 — Diagnose scale economies

**Problem.** A manufacturer’s least-cost long-run totals are €2.4m at 80,000 units, €3.6m at 150,000 units, and €6.0m at 200,000 units. Classify each expansion.

**Method.** Compute long-run average total cost.

**Step-by-step solution.** LRATC values are `€2.4m/80,000 = €30`, `€3.6m/150,000 = €24`, and `€6.0m/200,000 = €30`. Cost falls from €30 to €24, then rises to €30.

**Final answer.** Economies of scale through 150,000 units; diseconomies beyond that observed point.

**Interpretation.** The lowest observed efficient scale is 150,000 units.

**Exam insight.** Compare average cost, not total cost: total cost normally rises even under economies of scale.

> **Knowledge check 3.** Can a firm experience diminishing marginal returns in the short run while enjoying long-run economies of scale? **Answer:** Yes; they describe different horizons and input adjustments.

## 4. Four market structures

The classification depends on number of sellers, product differentiation, entry barriers, pricing power, and strategic interdependence—not on a single label.

| Structure | Sellers/product | Entry barriers | Firm demand and pricing power | Strategic interaction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Perfect competition | Many; identical product | Very low | Horizontal at market price; none | Minimal |
| Monopolistic competition | Many; differentiated substitutes | Low | Downward sloping; limited | Mostly through differentiation |
| Oligopoly | Few; identical or differentiated | High | Depends on rivals’ reactions | Central |
| Pure monopoly | One; no close substitute | Very high | Market demand; substantial, constrained by demand/regulation | No direct product-market rival |

Under perfect competition, the individual firm is a price taker, so `P = MR`. For an imperfect competitor with downward-sloping demand, selling more normally requires lowering price; therefore marginal revenue is below price. All structures still use marginal analysis, but strategic behavior and the shape of demand differ.

### Worked example 7 — Identify perfect competition

**Problem.** Hundreds of farms sell an undifferentiated crop at a quoted market price. Buyers switch suppliers at negligible cost and farms can enter next season. Identify the closest structure.

**Method.** Match seller count, differentiation, entry barriers, and pricing power.

**Step-by-step solution.** Sellers are numerous, the product is homogeneous, switching is easy, and entry barriers are low. A farm cannot raise price without losing buyers.

**Final answer.** Perfect competition is the closest model.

**Interpretation.** Each farm faces approximately horizontal demand at the market price.

**Exam insight.** Real markets need not satisfy an ideal perfectly; choose the structure best supported by the facts.

### Worked example 8 — Identify monopolistic competition

**Problem.** A city has many cafés. Each differentiates location, atmosphere, and menu; a new café can open with moderate capital. What structure best fits?

**Method.** Separate product differentiation from barriers and seller count.

**Step-by-step solution.** Many sellers and relatively accessible entry rule against oligopoly and monopoly. Differentiation rules against perfect competition.

**Final answer.** Monopolistic competition.

**Interpretation.** Each café has limited pricing power but faces close substitutes and entry pressure.

**Exam insight.** “Many firms” does not imply perfect competition when products are differentiated.

> **Knowledge check 4.** Which single feature most distinguishes oligopoly? **Answer:** Mutual interdependence—each firm must consider likely rival responses.

## 5. Monopolistic competition

A monopolistically competitive firm faces downward-sloping demand because its product is differentiated. It chooses the quantity where MR equals MC and then reads the highest price consumers will pay for that quantity from the demand curve. Confusing the MR curve with the demand curve produces the wrong price.

Differentiation can be real or perceived and may involve features, service, location, branding, or distribution. Price cuts can increase volume but apply to units that would otherwise sell at the higher price, explaining why MR lies below price. Low entry barriers tend to erode economic profit over time, though differentiation may preserve some advantage.

### Worked example 9 — Price and output under differentiated demand

**Problem.** A differentiated seller has demand `P = 70 − Q`, marginal revenue `MR = 70 − 2Q`, and marginal cost `MC = 10`. Find optimal quantity and price.

**Method.** Set MR equal to MC for quantity; substitute quantity into demand for price.

**Step-by-step solution.** `70 − 2Q = 10`, so `2Q = 60` and `Q* = 30`. Demand gives `P* = 70 − 30 = 40`.

**Final answer.** Optimal quantity is 30 and price is €40.

**Interpretation.** The €30 gap between price and MC reflects downward-sloping demand, not a calculation error.

**Exam insight.** Never use the MR equation to obtain the customer price.

### Worked example 10 — Evaluate a price promotion

**Problem.** A brand can sell 1,000 units at €50 or 1,200 units at €46. Variable cost is €25 per unit and fixed cost is unchanged. Which option produces more contribution?

**Method.** Compare `(P − V)Q` because fixed cost is common.

**Step-by-step solution.** Current contribution is `(€50 − €25) × 1,000 = €25,000`. Promotion contribution is `(€46 − €25) × 1,200 = €25,200`.

**Final answer.** The promotion adds €200 of contribution.

**Interpretation.** Higher volume barely offsets the lower margin; execution risk could overturn the small modeled benefit.

**Exam insight.** Revenue growth alone cannot establish that a price cut improves profit.

> **Knowledge check 5.** Why can advertising matter in monopolistic competition? **Answer:** It can alter perceived differentiation and the position or elasticity of firm demand.

## 6. Oligopoly and strategic behavior

In oligopoly, each firm’s best action depends on what rivals do. Outcomes may reflect quantity competition, price competition, product differentiation, repeated interaction, or explicit regulation. There is no single universal oligopoly demand curve.

A Nash equilibrium is a set of strategies in which no participant benefits from changing strategy alone, given the others’ strategies. It need not maximize joint industry profit or social welfare. In a Cournot-style model, firms choose quantities while treating rivals’ quantities as given; total output determines market price. A kinked-demand narrative explains price rigidity by assuming rivals match price cuts but not price increases, though it does not determine the initial price.

| Oligopoly idea | Strategic choice | Maintained belief | Key implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cournot | Quantity | rival quantity fixed for own decision | reaction functions jointly determine output |
| Price competition | Price | buyers shift toward lower price | intense rivalry can compress margins |
| Kinked demand | Price | cuts matched; increases not matched | price may be sticky around current level |
| Nash equilibrium | Any defined strategy | rivals keep equilibrium strategies | no profitable unilateral deviation |

### Worked example 11 — Find a Nash equilibrium

**Problem.** Two firms choose High or Low price. Profits `(A,B)` are: High/High `(8,8)`, High/Low `(2,11)`, Low/High `(11,2)`, Low/Low `(5,5)`. Identify the Nash equilibrium.

**Method.** Find each firm’s best response to each rival action.

**Step-by-step solution.** If B chooses High, A prefers Low (`11 > 8`); if B chooses Low, A prefers Low (`5 > 2`). By symmetry, Low is also B’s best response to either A action. Neither can improve alone from Low/Low.

**Final answer.** Low/Low with profits `(5,5)`.

**Interpretation.** High/High would create greater joint profit, but it is unstable without an enforceable coordination mechanism.

**Exam insight.** Nash equilibrium is about unilateral deviations, not the highest total payoff.

### Worked example 12 — Cournot reaction reasoning

**Problem.** Firm A’s best-response quantity is `qA = 60 − 0.5qB`; Firm B’s is `qB = 60 − 0.5qA`. Find the symmetric equilibrium.

**Method.** Symmetry implies `qA = qB = q`; solve the shared reaction equation.

**Step-by-step solution.** `q = 60 − 0.5q`, so `1.5q = 60` and `q = 40`. Total quantity is 80.

**Final answer.** Each produces 40; industry output is 80.

**Interpretation.** Each quantity is a best response to the other, so neither firm gains by changing alone.

**Exam insight.** Do not maximize the two firms’ combined profit unless the question describes coordinated behavior.

> **Knowledge check 6.** Does observing stable prices prove a cartel exists? **Answer:** No. Repeated interaction, kinked-demand incentives, contracts, regulation, or similar costs can also produce stability.

## 7. Concentration measures

The N-firm concentration ratio adds the market shares of the N largest firms: `CR_N = Σ s_i`. The Herfindahl–Hirschman Index sums squared market shares: `HHI = Σ s_i²`. When shares are percentages, HHI ranges toward 10,000 for a single firm; when shares are decimals, it ranges toward 1. Squaring gives greater weight to large firms.

### Worked example 13 — Concentration ratio and HHI

**Problem.** Five firms have shares of 35%, 25%, 18%, 12%, and 10%. Calculate CR4 and HHI using percentage shares.

**Method.** Add the four largest shares; separately sum all squared shares.

**Step-by-step solution.** `CR4 = 35 + 25 + 18 + 12 = 90%`. `HHI = 35² + 25² + 18² + 12² + 10² = 1,225 + 625 + 324 + 144 + 100 = 2,418`.

**Final answer.** CR4 is 90%; HHI is 2,418.

**Interpretation.** The market is concentrated, and HHI reveals the unequal distribution hidden by CR4 alone.

**Exam insight.** State the share convention; 2,418 and 0.2418 describe the same structure under different scaling.

### Worked example 14 — Merger effect on HHI

**Problem.** In the preceding market, the 12% and 10% firms merge. What is the change in HHI?

**Method.** For two merging firms, `ΔHHI = 2ab` when shares use the same scale.

**Step-by-step solution.** `ΔHHI = 2 × 12 × 10 = 240`. New HHI is `2,418 + 240 = 2,658`.

**Final answer.** HHI rises by 240 to 2,658.

**Interpretation.** Combining previously separate shares increases the weight on concentration.

**Exam insight.** The shortcut works because `(a+b)² − a² − b² = 2ab`.

Concentration is evidence, not a verdict. Results depend on defining the product and geographic market. A high statistic may coexist with vigorous potential entry, rapid innovation, buyer power, or import competition. A low statistic may conceal coordinated behavior or common ownership. Historical accounting shares can lag current competitive conditions.

> **Knowledge check 7.** Two markets have identical CR4. Must they have identical HHI? **Answer:** No. HHI also reflects how shares are distributed within and beyond the top four.

## 8. Analyst decision workflow

1. Define the product and geographic market.
2. Identify substitutes, switching costs, and differentiation.
3. Assess current and potential sellers and entry barriers.
4. Examine unit economics: price, AVC, ATC, MR, and MC.
5. For a few-firm market, model credible rival responses.
6. Calculate concentration using a clearly stated share convention.
7. Stress-test the conclusion against regulation, technology, buyer power, and market-definition alternatives.

> **Knowledge check 8.** What is the most dangerous shortcut in structure analysis? **Answer:** Inferring pricing power from seller count or concentration alone without testing entry, substitutes, and strategic conditions.

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Using ATC instead of AVC for the short-run shutdown decision.
- Calling zero economic profit a failure rather than a normal opportunity-cost return.
- Finding quantity from demand rather than the `MR = MC` condition.
- Reading price from MR instead of demand for an imperfect competitor.
- Assuming a monopolist can charge any price; demand still constrains price and quantity.
- Treating all oligopolies as cartels or assuming one universal model.
- Selecting the jointly best payoff rather than testing unilateral deviations for Nash equilibrium.
- Comparing HHI values calculated with different share scales.
- Treating concentration as direct proof of market power.
- Confusing short-run diminishing returns with long-run diseconomies of scale.

## Memory aids

- **Break even on Total; Shut down on Variable.** ATC governs breakeven, AVC governs shutdown.
- **M before D:** use **M**R = MC for quantity, then **D**emand for price.
- **Many–Many–Few–One:** perfect competition, monopolistic competition, oligopoly, monopoly.
- **HHI highlights heavyweights:** squaring shares magnifies large firms.
- **Nash = no solo switch:** no player improves by changing alone.

## Exam tips

- Write the decision threshold before inserting numbers.
- With a cost table, use marginal comparisons and confirm MC is rising through MR.
- Under imperfect competition, separate output choice from price lookup.
- In game tables, mark best responses for both players before choosing an equilibrium.
- For concentration, define the market and share scale before interpreting the statistic.

## 30-second summary

Profit is maximized by comparing marginal revenue with marginal cost. Breakeven means total cost is covered; shutdown asks only whether operating revenue covers variable cost in the short run. Long-run average cost reveals economies or diseconomies of scale. Perfect competition has price-taking firms, monopolistic competition adds differentiation, oligopoly adds strategic interdependence, and monopoly has one protected seller. Imperfect competitors choose output with MR = MC and obtain price from demand. CR_N and HHI summarize concentration, but market definition, entry, substitutes, and conduct determine what the numbers mean.
