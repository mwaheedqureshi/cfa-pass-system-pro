# International Trade

## Chapter overview

This chapter examines why countries trade, what trade costs the groups who lose from it, how governments restrict trade with tariffs, quotas, export subsidies, and voluntary export restraints (VERs), and why countries form trading blocs of increasing depth — from a free trade area up to a full monetary union. The chapter closes by connecting each of these mechanisms to their effect on investment decisions.

## Why it matters

Trade policy changes move prices, production, and consumption in every country a company sources from or sells into. An analyst who can classify a proposed policy correctly, compute its welfare effect, and judge whether a regional bloc will help or hurt a specific firm's competitive position makes sharper calls on that firm's revenue, costs, and valuation than an analyst who only reacts to trade-policy headlines.

## Official 2027 learning outcomes

The curriculum metadata above contains the authorized outcomes. In practical terms, be ready to:

- describe the benefits and costs of international trade;
- compare types of trade restrictions, such as tariffs, quotas, and export subsidies, and their economic implications; and
- explain motivations for and advantages of trading blocs, common markets, and economic unions.

## Prerequisites

Basic supply-and-demand reasoning and the concept of consumer/producer surplus are useful background for the welfare-effect calculations in this chapter.

## 1. The benefits and costs of international trade

The most-cited arguments for international trade are that countries **gain from exchange and specialization**, industries gain **economies of scale**, households and firms gain **product variety**, **competition increases**, and **resources are allocated more efficiently**.

**Gains from exchange** arise because trade lets a country receive a higher price for what it exports and pay a lower price for what it imports than it could achieve by producing everything domestically. That reallocates production toward each country's export good and away from its import good, which raises the total bundle of goods available to consume — increasing overall welfare. Trade increasing *overall* welfare does not mean every individual consumer and producer is better off; it means the winners could, in theory, compensate the losers and still come out ahead.

**Comparative advantage** is the deeper source of gains from specialization. Two classical frameworks explain it: the **Ricardian model**, which traces comparative advantage to differences in technology between countries, and the **Heckscher–Ohlin model**, which traces it to differences in relative factor endowments (labor, capital, land). A country holds a comparative advantage in a good if its *opportunity cost* of producing that good — measured in units of the other good forgone — is lower than another country's opportunity cost for the same good. This is distinct from **absolute advantage**, which simply compares which country can produce more of a good with the same resources; a country can lack an absolute advantage in every good and still gain from trade if it specializes according to comparative advantage.

Newer trade models add three further sources of gain that do not depend on comparative-advantage differences at all: **economies of scale** (average production cost falls as output rises, benefiting industries such as automobiles and steel as the market they can sell into grows), **product variety** (two similar countries can still gain from trade if each specializes in different varieties of a differentiated good and they exchange those varieties — this is **intra-industry trade**, two-way trade within the same product category), and **increased competition** (foreign entrants erode domestic firms' monopoly power and force them toward greater efficiency). Trade liberalization is also associated with higher real GDP, through more efficient resource allocation, learning-by-doing (unit costs falling as cumulative output and experience rise), knowledge spillovers, and trade-induced improvements in the policies and institutions that support innovation.

**Costs of trade.** Opening to trade also imposes real costs. As an economy moves toward free trade, resources must be reallocated out of import-competing industries and into exporting industries; less-efficient firms in the contracting industry may exit, displacing workers who need retraining to find jobs elsewhere. These costs are concentrated in the short and medium run; in the long run, displaced resources are generally expected to be redeployed more productively — but workers whose industry-specific skills lose value may never fully recover, so "gains from trade" being positive in aggregate does not mean every stakeholder benefits, even eventually.

### Worked example 1 — Gains from exchange, qualitatively

**Problem.** Country Nordfjord has a lower cost of producing wind-turbine components than Country Sarrat, while Sarrat has a lower cost of producing processed coffee. Explain, without calculating anything, how trade between them raises the combined bundle of goods available to consume.

**Method.** Apply the gains-from-exchange logic.

**Step-by-step solution.** If each country shifts production toward the good it produces more cheaply (turbines for Nordfjord, coffee for Sarrat) and trades for the other good, each country pays a lower effective price for its import than it would cost to produce domestically, freeing up resources that can produce more of the export good instead.

**Final answer.** Both countries can consume a larger combined bundle of turbines and coffee than either could produce alone at the same resource cost.

**Interpretation.** This is the gains-from-exchange argument in its simplest form, independent of any specific numeric calculation.

**Exam insight.** "Gains from trade" describes an aggregate outcome; it does not assert that every producer or consumer individually benefits.

### Worked example 2 — Comparative advantage from opportunity cost

**Problem.** Country Vestland can produce either 1 unit of textiles using 4 labor-hours or 1 unit of semiconductors using 8 labor-hours. Country Eastmark can produce either 1 unit of textiles using 3 labor-hours or 1 unit of semiconductors using 3 labor-hours. Determine each country's comparative advantage.

**Method.** Compute each country's opportunity cost of textiles in terms of semiconductors forgone, then compare.

**Step-by-step solution.** Vestland's opportunity cost of 1 textile unit = 4/8 = 0.50 semiconductor units forgone. Eastmark's opportunity cost of 1 textile unit = 3/3 = 1.00 semiconductor unit forgone. Vestland gives up fewer semiconductors per textile unit, so Vestland has the lower opportunity cost — and the comparative advantage — in textiles. Eastmark, by elimination, has the comparative advantage in semiconductors (its opportunity cost of a semiconductor, 3/3 = 1.00 textile unit, is lower than Vestland's, 8/4 = 2.00 textile units).

**Final answer.** Vestland: comparative advantage in textiles. Eastmark: comparative advantage in semiconductors.

**Interpretation.** Eastmark is more efficient than Vestland at producing *both* goods in absolute terms (fewer labor-hours each), yet both countries still gain by specializing according to comparative, not absolute, advantage.

**Exam insight.** Never determine comparative advantage by comparing raw labor-hours across countries directly — always compute each country's own internal opportunity cost first.

### Worked example 3 — Short-run versus long-run adjustment

**Problem.** Country Rivermoor has a lower relative cost of producing ceramics than Country Baytown, which has a lower relative cost of producing furniture. The two countries open to free trade. Describe the short-run and long-run effect on Baytown's ceramics industry.

**Method.** Apply the industry-adjustment reasoning to the import-competing sector.

**Step-by-step solution.** Because ceramics are relatively cheap to produce in Rivermoor, Baytown's domestic ceramics industry will contract as cheaper imports arrive; in the short run, ceramics workers in Baytown may become unemployed. In the long run, displaced workers with transferable skills are expected to find jobs in Baytown's expanding furniture-export industry, though workers whose skills are ceramics-specific may remain permanently worse off.

**Final answer.** Short run: contraction and some unemployment in Baytown's ceramics industry. Long run: most displaced resources migrate to the expanding furniture industry, but not every displaced worker recovers fully.

**Interpretation.** The overall welfare effect on both countries is positive even though it is not evenly distributed, and even the long run does not guarantee every individual stakeholder is made better off.

**Exam insight.** "Gains from trade" is an aggregate, not a unanimous, result — expect scenario questions that test this distinction directly.

### Worked example 4 — Economies of scale from a larger market

**Problem.** A semiconductor manufacturer's average cost per unit falls as its total production volume rises. Before trade, the manufacturer sells only domestically. After its home country opens to trade, the manufacturer begins exporting as well. Explain the effect on its average production cost.

**Method.** Apply the economies-of-scale argument for industries with increasing returns to scale.

**Step-by-step solution.** Trade expands the market the manufacturer can sell into beyond its home market; producing a larger total volume across the larger combined market allows the manufacturer to move further down its declining average-cost curve.

**Final answer.** Average production cost per unit falls as the addressable market — and therefore output — grows through trade.

**Interpretation.** This gain is available even to two countries with identical technology and factor endowments, unlike comparative-advantage-based gains.

**Exam insight.** Distinguish economies-of-scale gains (from market size) from comparative-advantage gains (from cost differences) — both are valid, independent sources of gains from trade.

### Worked example 5 — Recognizing intra-industry trade

**Problem.** Country Halworth both exports and imports passenger cars — it exports compact cars and imports luxury sedans, both classified within the same "passenger vehicles" product category. Classify this trade pattern and explain why it can occur between similar countries.

**Method.** Apply the intra-industry-trade definition and its monopolistic-competition rationale.

**Step-by-step solution.** Two-way trade within the same product classification is intra-industry trade. It arises because firms in a differentiated-product industry each specialize in producing and exporting particular varieties while importing other varieties, letting consumers in both countries access a wider range of varieties and firms capture greater economies of scale from serving a combined market.

**Final answer.** This is intra-industry trade, explained by product differentiation and specialization by variety rather than by comparative advantage.

**Interpretation.** Intra-industry trade is common between economically similar countries precisely because it does not require an underlying cost-based comparative advantage.

**Exam insight.** A scenario describing simultaneous imports and exports within one product category, between similar economies, is the signature pattern of intra-industry trade — do not force a comparative-advantage explanation onto it.

### Worked example 6 — Knowledge spillovers and long-run growth

**Problem.** A firm relocates a production facility to a country with a highly skilled labor force, an established supplier base, and a growing local industry in the same product category, in order to win a large manufacturing contract. Explain, in the module's own terms, how this relocation could contribute to that country's long-run growth beyond the immediate jobs created.

**Method.** Apply the knowledge-spillover and learning-by-doing channels linking trade to GDP growth.

**Step-by-step solution.** Beyond the direct jobs and output, the facility can generate learning-by-doing (falling unit costs as workers and managers gain experience), exchange of technical expertise with local suppliers, and broader knowledge spillovers into the local industry — all channels through which openness to trade is associated with higher long-run real GDP growth, alongside more efficient resource allocation generally.

**Final answer.** Learning-by-doing and knowledge-spillover effects can raise local productivity and long-run growth beyond the immediate contract.

**Interpretation.** These growth channels are cited as associated with — not mechanically guaranteed by — greater openness to trade; the module describes the relationship's strength as still debated.

**Exam insight.** Do not overstate the trade–growth link as certain or precisely quantifiable; the module frames it as a debated, evidence-supported association with several named channels, not a fixed formula.

> **Knowledge check 1.** Name the five most-cited arguments for the benefits of trade. **Answer:** Gains from exchange and specialization, economies of scale, product variety, increased competition, and more efficient resource allocation.

## 2. Trade restrictions: tariffs, quotas, and export subsidies

**Trade restrictions** (trade protection) are government policies that limit the ability of domestic households and firms to trade freely with other countries. Named examples include tariffs, import quotas, VERs, export subsidies, embargoes, and domestic content requirements. Governments impose them to protect established domestic industries from foreign competition, protect infant industries until they mature, protect domestic employment, protect strategic industries for national security, raise government revenue (particularly in developing countries), or retaliate against another country's own restrictions.

**Capital restrictions** are a distinct category: controls on foreigners' ability to own domestic assets, or on domestic residents' ability to own foreign assets. Trade restrictions limit the openness of *goods* markets; capital restrictions limit the openness of *financial* markets.

### Tariffs

A **tariff** is a tax a government levies on imported goods, primarily to protect domestic industries producing the same or similar goods (and sometimes to narrow a trade deficit). A tariff raises the domestic price above the free-trade world price, reducing import demand.

The analysis assumes a **small country** — one that is a price taker in the world market for the good (not necessarily small by population or GDP; by many measures Brazil is a large country but is a price taker for cars). A **large country**, by contrast, is a large enough importer to move the world price: when it imposes a tariff, the exporter may lower its price to retain market share, shifting the terms of trade in the large country's favor. In theory, a large country can gain from imposing a tariff if (1) its trading partner does not retaliate and (2) the resulting deadweight loss is smaller than the terms-of-trade benefit — but global welfare still falls, because the large country's gain cannot exceed the loss it imposes on its partner.

### Welfare effects of a tariff or quota

```text
      Price
        │                    Domestic
        │                    Supply
   Pt   │        ┌──A──┬──B──┐
        │        │     │/////│
   P*   │────────┴─────┴─────┴────── Domestic
        │       /│//////│    │       Demand
        │      / │  C   │ D  │
        │     /  │//////│    │
        └────┴───┴──────┴────┴────── Quantity
             Qs_ft  Qs_r  Qd_r  Qd_ft
```

At the world price P*, domestic supply is `Qs_ft` and domestic demand is `Qd_ft`, so imports equal `Qd_ft − Qs_ft`. After a restriction raises the domestic price to `Pt`, domestic supply rises to `Qs_r` and domestic demand falls to `Qd_r`, so imports shrink to `Qd_r − Qs_r`.

| Effect | Region(s) | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer surplus | A + B + C + D | Decreases |
| Producer surplus | A | Increases |
| Tariff revenue or quota rent | C | Captured by government (tariff), or ambiguous — captured domestically or by the foreign exporter (quota) |
| National welfare | −(B + D) | Decreases (deadweight loss) |

Consumer surplus falls by the full trapezoid A+B+C+D because consumers pay a higher price on every unit still purchased. Producer surplus rises by rectangle+triangle A. Region C — either tariff revenue or quota rent — is a *transfer*, not a net loss to the country as a whole, as long as it stays domestic. Regions B and D are genuine **deadweight loss**: B is production-side inefficiency (resources drawn into production that costs more than the world price), and D is consumption-side inefficiency (consumers willing to pay between P* and Pt are priced out of a mutually beneficial exchange).

### Worked example 7 — Full tariff welfare calculation

**Problem.** Country Kestrel produces 400,000 meters of denim domestically and consumes 700,000 meters at the world price of USD2.00 per meter (importing the 300,000-meter difference). Kestrel's government imposes a tariff that raises the domestic price to USD2.50 per meter. At the new price, domestic production rises to 460,000 meters and domestic consumption falls to 640,000 meters. Calculate the loss in consumer surplus, the gain in producer surplus, the government's tariff revenue, and the deadweight loss.

**Method.** Apply the four welfare-effect relationships with ΔP = Pt − P*, `Qs_ft` = 400,000, `Qs_r` = 460,000, `Qd_r` = 640,000, `Qd_ft` = 700,000.

**Step-by-step solution.**
ΔP = USD2.50 − USD2.00 = USD0.50.
Consumer surplus loss = ΔP × Qd_r + ½ × ΔP × (Qd_ft − Qd_r) = (0.50 × 640,000) + ½ × 0.50 × (700,000 − 640,000) = 320,000 + 15,000 = USD335,000.
Producer surplus gain = ΔP × Qs_ft + ½ × ΔP × (Qs_r − Qs_ft) = (0.50 × 400,000) + ½ × 0.50 × (460,000 − 400,000) = 200,000 + 15,000 = USD215,000.
Government tariff revenue = ΔP × (Qd_r − Qs_r) = 0.50 × (640,000 − 460,000) = 0.50 × 180,000 = USD90,000.
Deadweight loss = ½ × ΔP × (Qs_r − Qs_ft) + ½ × ΔP × (Qd_ft − Qd_r) = (½ × 0.50 × 60,000) + (½ × 0.50 × 60,000) = 15,000 + 15,000 = USD30,000.

**Final answer.** CS loss = USD335,000; PS gain = USD215,000; tariff revenue = USD90,000; deadweight loss = USD30,000. (Check: 335,000 = 215,000 + 90,000 + 30,000, confirming the trapezoid decomposes exactly into the three effects.)

**Interpretation.** The CS loss exceeds the sum of the PS gain and government revenue precisely by the deadweight loss — the tariff has no way to avoid this efficiency cost even though most of the CS loss is merely a transfer to producers and the government.

**Exam insight.** Always check that CS loss = PS gain + revenue/rent + deadweight loss; the identity is a fast way to catch an arithmetic error.

### Quotas and voluntary export restraints (VERs)

A **quota** restricts the quantity of a good that can be imported, typically for a specified period, administered through import licenses. A quota that restricts imports to the same quantity as an equivalent tariff produces an *identical* price increase and identical regions A, B, C, D — but who captures region C (now called the **quota rent** rather than tariff revenue) is ambiguous: it may go to the domestic government (if import licenses are auctioned), to domestic importers, or to the foreign exporter (who can raise its own price under the restricted quantity). If the rent leaks abroad, national welfare loss expands from B+D to B+C+D — worse than the equivalent tariff.

A **VER** is functionally close to a quota but is imposed by the *exporting* country rather than the importer. Because the exporter administers it, the VER's rent is captured by the exporter (or exporting country) by construction — so a VER produces the largest welfare loss (B+C+D) for the importing country of the three restriction types, with no ambiguity about where the rent goes.

### Worked example 8 — Same tariff, reframed as a quota

**Problem.** Using the Country Kestrel figures from Worked example 7, suppose the government instead imposes an import quota that restricts imports to the same 180,000 meters (rather than a tariff), producing the same domestic price of USD2.50. Compare the national welfare loss if (a) the government auctions the import licenses and keeps the proceeds, versus (b) foreign exporters capture the quota rent.

**Method.** Apply the quota-rent-capture distinction to the same regions A, B, C, D computed in Worked example 7.

**Step-by-step solution.** The price change and quantities are identical to the tariff case, so regions A, B, C, D are unchanged: B+D = USD30,000 (the deadweight loss computed above), and C = USD90,000 (the same USD90,000 previously computed as tariff revenue, now called the quota rent). (a) If the government auctions the licenses, it captures C domestically, so national welfare loss = B+D = USD30,000, identical to the tariff. (b) If foreign exporters capture the rent instead, national welfare loss = B+C+D = USD30,000 + USD90,000 = USD120,000.

**Final answer.** (a) USD30,000 national welfare loss (same as the tariff). (b) USD120,000 national welfare loss (USD90,000 worse than the tariff).

**Interpretation.** A quota is never welfare-superior to the equivalent tariff for the importing country; at best it ties, and at worst — if rent leaks to foreign exporters — it is substantially worse.

**Exam insight.** A tariff always keeps region C at home (as government revenue); a quota's region C is domestically captured only under specific administration (license auctions) — never assume a quota automatically matches a tariff's welfare outcome.

### Worked example 9 — VER versus quota, from an investor's perspective

**Problem.** An analyst is evaluating a domestic importing firm in a small country that currently benefits from partially capturing quota rents under an existing import quota. The government is considering replacing the quota with a VER negotiated directly with foreign exporters. Would this change benefit or hurt the importing firm?

**Method.** Apply the quota-versus-VER rent-capture distinction.

**Step-by-step solution.** Under the VER, the exporting country administers the restriction, so all of the rent (region C) is captured by foreign exporters by construction — the domestic importing firm loses the portion of the rent it was previously able to capture under the quota.

**Final answer.** The change would hurt the importing firm; a VER is worse for it than a quota under which it captured at least part of the rent.

**Interpretation.** Which party administers a quantity restriction — the importing country (quota) or the exporting country (VER) — directly determines who can plausibly capture the rent.

**Exam insight.** "Who administers the restriction" is the single fastest way to distinguish a quota from a VER in a scenario question.

### Export subsidies

An **export subsidy** is a per-unit payment from the government to a firm for each unit of a good it exports, intended to stimulate exports. It distorts production and trade away from comparative advantage and therefore reduces welfare. In the exporting country, the subsidy gives producers an incentive to shift sales from the domestic market to the export market, since they now receive the world price plus the subsidy on exported units; in the small-country case this raises the *domestic* price by the amount of the subsidy (domestic buyers must now match the higher effective price producers can get by exporting). Domestic production rises and domestic consumption falls, while exports increase. In the large-country case, increased exports push the *world* price down, meaning part of the subsidy is effectively transferred to the foreign country — a small country cannot achieve this transfer. The net welfare effect is negative in both cases, with a larger loss for the large country. Governments importing subsidized goods may respond with **countervailing duties** — duties levied specifically against subsidized imports.

### Comparing the four restriction types

| Effect | Tariff | Import quota | Export subsidy | VER |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Producer surplus | Increases | Increases | Increases | Increases |
| Consumer surplus | Decreases | Decreases | Decreases | Decreases |
| Government revenue | Increases | Mixed (depends on who captures the rent) | Falls (government spending rises) | No change (rent to foreign exporters) |
| National welfare | Decreases (small country); could increase (large country) | Decreases (small country); could increase (large country) | Decreases | Decreases |
| Price | Increases | Increases | Increases (in the exporting country) | Increases |
| Domestic consumption | Decreases | Decreases | Decreases | Decreases |
| Domestic production | Increases | Increases | Increases | Increases |
| Trade | Imports decrease | Imports decrease | Exports increase | Imports decrease |

All four restriction types share the same price-and-quantity direction (price up, consumption down, production up) despite targeting opposite sides of the trade account — the difference between them lies almost entirely in who captures the price wedge (government, ambiguous, foreign exporter, or nobody) and therefore how large the resulting national welfare loss is.

### Worked example 10 — Export subsidy effects

**Problem.** Country Halberg, a small exporter of soybeans, introduces a per-unit export subsidy. Describe the effect on Halberg's domestic soybean price, domestic production, domestic consumption, and government spending.

**Method.** Apply the small-country export-subsidy mechanism.

**Step-by-step solution.** Producers now earn the world price plus the subsidy on every exported unit, so they redirect sales from the domestic market toward exports until the domestic price rises to match this higher effective export return; domestic production rises in response to the higher effective price; domestic consumption falls as the domestic price rises; government spending rises, since the subsidy is a per-unit payment out of the government's budget (unlike a tariff, which raises revenue).

**Final answer.** Domestic price rises, domestic production rises, domestic consumption falls, exports rise, and government spending (not revenue) rises.

**Interpretation.** An export subsidy moves prices and quantities in the same *directions* as a tariff or quota (price up, consumption down, production up) despite being aimed at the opposite side of the trade account (encouraging exports rather than restricting imports).

**Exam insight.** Do not assume an export subsidy lowers the domestic price just because it is meant to help foreign buyers — the small-country mechanism raises the domestic price.

### Worked example 11 — When might a large country gain from a tariff?

**Problem.** Explain the specific, narrow condition under which a large country's tariff could raise its own national welfare, and why this does not contradict the general deadweight-loss result for tariffs.

**Method.** Apply the large-country terms-of-trade argument.

**Step-by-step solution.** If the exporting country lowers its price in response to the large country's tariff (to retain market share) and does not retaliate, the large country's terms-of-trade gain can, in principle, exceed its own deadweight loss, raising the large importing country's own national welfare. This does not contradict the general result because the terms-of-trade gain is a *transfer* from the exporting country, not new global output — global welfare still falls, since the large country's gain is smaller than the loss it imposes on its trading partner.

**Final answer.** A large-country tariff can raise the imposing country's own welfare only if the terms-of-trade gain from the exporter's price cut exceeds its own deadweight loss and the partner does not retaliate; global welfare still declines.

**Interpretation.** This is the one documented exception to "a tariff always reduces national welfare" — and it applies only to a large country's own welfare, never to global welfare.

**Exam insight.** A question asking whether a tariff "can ever raise welfare" is testing the large-country/terms-of-trade exception specifically — a small country (price taker) has no such exception.

### Worked example 12 — Matching restrictions to their government-revenue effect

**Problem.** For each of the following four trade restrictions, state the effect on the importing (or, for the export subsidy, exporting) country's government budget: a tariff, an import quota, an export subsidy, a VER.

**Method.** Apply each restriction's revenue/spending mechanism.

**Step-by-step solution.** Tariff: government revenue increases (it collects the per-unit tax). Import quota: mixed — revenue increases only if the government auctions the import licenses; otherwise the rent may go to importers or foreign exporters, so there is no guaranteed revenue effect. Export subsidy: government spending increases (it pays out a per-unit subsidy) — this is a cost, not revenue. VER: no government revenue effect at all — the rent goes to foreign exporters by construction.

**Final answer.** Tariff: revenue up. Quota: mixed/ambiguous. Export subsidy: spending up (net cost). VER: no government revenue effect.

**Interpretation.** Government-revenue effect is one of the fastest ways to distinguish the four restriction types in a scenario question.

**Exam insight.** Memorize this row before memorizing the welfare-magnitude ranking — it is tested more literally and has no ambiguity once you know which restriction is described.

### Worked example 13 — Domestic content requirements and embargoes

**Problem.** A government requires that at least 40% of the value added in a locally sold vehicle originate domestically, and separately imposes a complete ban on importing a specific good from one particular country. Classify each policy.

**Method.** Match each described policy to its named trade-restriction category.

**Step-by-step solution.** A minimum-local-value-added rule for goods sold domestically is a **domestic content requirement**. A complete prohibition on trade with a specific country is an **embargo**.

**Final answer.** First policy: domestic content requirement. Second policy: embargo.

**Interpretation.** Both are named, real categories of trade restriction alongside tariffs, quotas, VERs, and export subsidies, even though this module's numeric welfare framework is developed in most detail for tariffs and quotas specifically.

**Exam insight.** Do not assume the welfare-region framework (A, B, C, D) applies mechanically to every restriction type — it is developed explicitly for tariffs and quotas, which share an identical price-and-quantity effect.

### Worked example 14 — Capital restriction or trade restriction?

**Problem.** A government caps the share of a domestic company's equity that non-resident investors may own. Is this a trade restriction or a capital restriction?

**Method.** Apply the trade-restriction-versus-capital-restriction distinction.

**Step-by-step solution.** A cap on foreign ownership of domestic assets (equity) limits the openness of the financial market, not the goods market.

**Final answer.** A capital restriction.

**Interpretation.** Trade restrictions and capital restrictions are both government-imposed limits on cross-border economic activity, but they operate on different markets (goods versus financial assets) and are analyzed separately.

**Exam insight.** A question describing limits on cross-border *ownership* or *asset* flows — rather than limits on the flow of *goods* — is testing capital restrictions, a distinct category from this chapter's tariff/quota/subsidy framework.

> **Knowledge check 2.** Rank the tariff, quota (rent captured abroad), and VER by their welfare loss to the importing country, from smallest to largest. **Answer:** Tariff (B+D) ≤ quota with foreign-captured rent (B+C+D) ≈ VER (B+C+D); a quota with domestically auctioned rent ties the tariff at B+D.

## 3. Trading blocs and regional integration

A **regional trading bloc** (regional trade agreement, RTA) is a group of countries that has agreed to reduce and progressively eliminate barriers to trade — and, in deeper forms, to the movement of factors of production — among its members. Regional integration has grown popular because eliminating barriers among a small group of countries is politically less contentious and faster than multilateral negotiation through the World Trade Organization (WTO), the global forum for negotiating trade rules and settling trade disputes between nations.

### The integration ladder

| Level | What is added | Real-world example named in the source |
|---|---|---|
| Free trade area (FTA) | Barriers to goods/services trade eliminated among members; each member keeps its own policy toward non-members | USMCA (United States, Canada, Mexico) |
| Customs union | + a common trade policy (common external tariff) toward non-members | Benelux (Belgium, Netherlands, Luxembourg), formed 1947 |
| Common market | + free movement of factors of production (labor, capital) among members | MERCOSUR (Argentina, Brazil, Paraguay, Uruguay) |
| Economic union | + common economic institutions and coordinated economic policy among members | The European Union (from 1993) |
| Monetary union | + a shared common currency | The eurozone, a subset of EU members sharing the euro |

Each level is **cumulative**: a customs union is an FTA plus a common external tariff; a common market is a customs union plus free factor mobility; an economic union is a common market plus shared institutions and policy coordination; a monetary union additionally requires a shared currency, which is only possible on top of the deeper economic-union level of coordination.

### Worked example 15 — Classifying an integration level

**Problem.** Countries Larkspur and Meridian eliminate all tariffs on each other's goods and allow free movement of labor and capital between them, but each still sets its own tariff independently against every other country in the world. Classify their arrangement, and explain why it is not a customs union.

**Method.** Apply the cumulative integration-ladder definitions.

**Step-by-step solution.** Free movement of goods and factors of production between members, without a common external tariff, describes a **common market** — but the *absence* of a common external tariff means it is specifically not a customs union, which requires that shared external policy.

**Final answer.** A common market; it fails the customs-union test because each country still sets its own external tariff independently.

**Interpretation.** A common market's deeper factor-mobility feature does not itself imply a common external tariff — the two features are independent additions on the ladder, both required for a customs union to also qualify as (or exceed) a common market.

**Exam insight.** Always check for the *specific* combination of features named in a scenario rather than assuming "more integration described" automatically means "customs union" — a common market lacking a common external tariff is a distinct, valid classification.

### Regional integration, trade creation, and trade diversion

Regional integration shifts trade patterns in two distinct ways, both effects of forming a customs union or deeper bloc:

- **Trade creation** occurs when regional integration replaces higher-cost domestic production with lower-cost imports from a fellow member — a pure efficiency gain.
- **Trade diversion** occurs when regional integration replaces lower-cost imports from a non-member with higher-cost imports from a member, because the non-member still faces the bloc's external tariff while the member does not — an efficiency loss.

```text
   Before bloc: import from lowest-cost source, wherever it is (member or non-member)
                              │
             Bloc forms; external tariff removed for members only
                              │
        ┌─────────────────────┴─────────────────────┐
        ▼                                             ▼
  TRADE CREATION                                TRADE DIVERSION
  Cheaper member import replaces          Cheaper non-member import replaced
  costlier domestic production            by costlier member import (which
  → efficiency GAIN                       is now tariff-free) → efficiency LOSS
```

Both effects typically occur together when a bloc forms; if trade creation exceeds trade diversion, the net welfare effect of the bloc is positive — but this is not guaranteed in every case.

### Worked example 16 — Trade creation

**Problem.** Country Anvale produces 8 million units of a good domestically and imports 3 million units from fellow (future) bloc member Bergholt, which has a lower production cost; Anvale applies a 12% tariff on these imports. After Anvale and Bergholt form a customs union eliminating tariffs between them, Anvale's domestic production falls to 5 million units and its imports from Bergholt rise to 9 million units. How much of the change in imports is trade creation (replacing domestic production) versus new consumption?

**Method.** Compare the change in domestic production to the total change in imports.

**Step-by-step solution.** Anvale's domestic production fell by 8 million − 5 million = 3 million units; this 3 million units of formerly domestic production is now supplied by imports from Bergholt instead — trade creation. Total imports rose from 3 million to 9 million, a 6-million-unit increase; of that increase, 3 million units is the trade-creation replacement of domestic production, and the remaining 3 million units reflects new consumption made possible by the lower post-union price.

**Final answer.** 3 million units of the import increase is trade creation (replacing domestic production); the other 3 million units is new consumption from the lower price.

**Interpretation.** Trade creation is measured specifically by the decline in domestic production being replaced by member imports — not by the full increase in imports, part of which reflects genuinely new demand.

**Exam insight.** Do not equate "increase in imports after bloc formation" with "trade creation" — only the portion that displaces prior domestic production counts as trade creation; the rest is a separate, additional consumption effect.

### Worked example 17 — Trade diversion

**Problem.** Before forming a customs union, Country Danholm imports a good from Country Estmere (the lowest-cost global producer) despite a 12% tariff that applies equally to all non-member imports, because Estmere is still cheaper than any domestic or bloc-partner alternative even after the tariff. Danholm then forms a customs union with Country Farrow, eliminating the tariff on Farrow's exports only while keeping the 12% tariff on Estmere. After the union, Danholm switches its imports entirely to Farrow. Is this trade creation or trade diversion, and why?

**Method.** Compare the true lowest-cost source (pre-tariff) to the source actually chosen after the union.

**Step-by-step solution.** Estmere remains the lowest-cost producer in absolute terms; Danholm only switches away from Estmere because the customs union's asymmetric tariff treatment (zero for Farrow, 12% for Estmere) makes Farrow's after-tariff price lower than Estmere's after-tariff price, even though Farrow is not the true lowest-cost producer.

**Final answer.** Trade diversion — Danholm replaces a genuinely lower-cost non-member source (Estmere) with a higher-cost member source (Farrow), purely because of the bloc's tariff asymmetry.

**Interpretation.** Trade diversion is an efficiency loss precisely because the switch is driven by the tariff structure, not by genuine relative production cost.

**Exam insight.** The tell for trade diversion is a country switching import sources *after* a bloc forms, away from a non-member that was cheaper before any tariffs existed — the union's own tariff asymmetry, not a genuine cost advantage, drives the switch.

### Costs, benefits, and challenges of deeper integration

Regional blocs largely extend the benefits of free trade generally (comparative-advantage specialization, reduced monopoly power, economies of scale, learning-by-doing, technology transfer, knowledge spillovers, greater foreign investment, and better-priced intermediate inputs), plus two bloc-specific benefits: reduced potential for conflict through greater interdependence among members, and greater collective bargaining power and political influence than members would have acting individually. Highly integrated groups of countries also tend to show stronger cross-border growth spillovers — well-integrated economies transmit good growth outcomes to each other more readily than poorly integrated ones.

Integration also imposes real costs: import competition can force inefficient firms to exit and displace workers, some of whom (especially those with narrow, industry-specific skills) may never find comparably paid work again, even though consumers typically gain from greater product variety and surviving firms typically become more productive. Concerns about **national sovereignty** — particularly when very differently sized economies join the same bloc — can also impede deeper integration.

Two further challenges specifically limit how far integration can deepen: (1) cultural and historical differences can complicate the political process of integration, and (2) a high degree of economic integration constrains members' ability to run independent economic and social policy, since free factor mobility and shared external commitments limit how far any one member can diverge from the group. This constraint is most binding in a **monetary union**, where monetary policy is no longer under any individual member's control and currency devaluation is unavailable as a tool to correct that member's own persistent imbalances — a vulnerability that can let one member's crisis spread to others sharing the same currency.

### Worked example 18 — Weighing adjustment costs against consumer gains

**Problem.** After two countries form a free trade area, one country's import-competing manufacturing sector loses jobs, generating estimated annual adjustment costs of USD1.2 billion, while consumers in that same country gain an estimated USD1.6 billion annually from access to a wider variety of imported goods at lower prices. Is the net national welfare effect, on this evidence, positive or negative — and what caveat applies to the comparison?

**Method.** Compare the stated benefit and cost estimates, and identify the module's own stated caveat about time-distribution of each effect.

**Step-by-step solution.** USD1.6 billion in annual consumer gains exceeds USD1.2 billion in annual adjustment costs, so the net effect on this evidence is positive. The caveat: consumer gains from added variety tend to persist and can grow over time as long as the trade relationship continues, while adjustment costs from job losses tend to decline over time as displaced workers find new employment — so a single year's comparison understates how the net benefit typically evolves.

**Final answer.** Net effect is positive (USD0.4 billion), and the true multi-year comparison is even more favorable, since consumer gains tend to persist or grow while adjustment costs tend to fade.

**Interpretation.** A single-year snapshot can understate the long-run case for trade liberalization if it does not account for each effect's different trajectory over time.

**Exam insight.** When a question presents a one-year cost-versus-benefit comparison for trade liberalization, check whether it is implicitly asking you to also reason about how each side evolves over subsequent years.

### Worked example 19 — A monetary union's sovereignty cost

**Problem.** Two member countries of a monetary union experience very different domestic economic conditions: one faces high unemployment and would normally cut interest rates and let its currency depreciate, while the other faces overheating and would normally raise rates. Explain why neither country can take its normal, independent policy response.

**Method.** Apply the monetary-union sovereignty constraint.

**Step-by-step solution.** Inside a monetary union, monetary policy (the interest-rate decision) is set once for the entire currency area, not separately by each member, and no member retains its own currency to devalue or revalue — so neither country can unilaterally cut or raise rates, or adjust its exchange rate, to address its own domestic conditions.

**Final answer.** Neither country can act independently; both are bound by the union's single monetary policy and shared currency, regardless of how different their domestic conditions are.

**Interpretation.** This loss of independent monetary and exchange-rate tools is the specific mechanism behind the module's broader point that deep integration constrains members' ability to run independent economic policy — and it is most severe in a monetary union specifically.

**Exam insight.** A scenario contrasting two very different economies stuck with one shared monetary policy is testing the monetary-union sovereignty cost — the same underlying trade-off tested more generally for deeper integration overall.

### Worked example 20 — Investment implications of joining a bloc

**Problem.** An analyst is evaluating whether to recommend increased investment in a country that has just joined a common market with several neighboring economies. List two considerations that support a more favorable view, and one consideration that tempers it.

**Method.** Apply the module's stated investment implications of regional integration.

**Step-by-step solution.** Supporting considerations: the cost of doing business is lower across a large, single regional market, and firms operating in the country can benefit from larger-scale economies as they serve the whole bloc rather than one national market. Tempering consideration: differences in tastes, culture, and competitive conditions persist even within a bloc and can limit the benefits actually realized from cross-border investment, and — depending on the depth of integration and available safeguards — problems in one member economy can spread relatively quickly to others in the bloc.

**Final answer.** Favorable: lower cost of doing business across the bloc; larger-scale economies of scale. Tempering: persistent cross-country differences in tastes/culture/competition, and contagion risk from other members.

**Interpretation.** Regional integration changes the *opportunity set* for investment, but does not eliminate country-specific due diligence or cross-border contagion risk.

**Exam insight.** A well-rounded investment-implications answer names both an upside (market access/scale) and a specific limitation (persistent differences, contagion risk) rather than treating bloc membership as an unambiguous positive.

> **Knowledge check 3.** What is the key difference between how a customs union and a common market treat factors of production? **Answer:** A customs union does not require free movement of factors of production among members; a common market adds that requirement on top of a customs union's shared external tariff.

## Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Comparative advantage | The ability to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost (not necessarily lower absolute cost) than another country. |
| Absolute advantage | The ability to produce more of a good with the same resources than another country. |
| Intra-industry trade | Two-way trade in goods within the same product category or classification. |
| Trade restriction | A government policy limiting the ability of domestic households and firms to trade freely with other countries. |
| Capital restriction | A government-imposed limit on foreign ownership of domestic assets, or domestic ownership of foreign assets. |
| Tariff | A tax a government levies on imported goods. |
| Small country (trade sense) | A country that is a price taker in the world market for a given good, regardless of its overall size. |
| Large country (trade sense) | A country large enough as an importer or exporter to influence the good's world price. |
| Quota | A government-imposed limit on the quantity of a good that may be imported, typically for a set period. |
| Quota rent | The profit captured on units still imported under a quota, arising from the quota-restricted price increase. |
| Voluntary export restraint (VER) | An exporter-imposed limit on the quantity of a good it will export to a trading partner. |
| Export subsidy | A government payment to a firm for each unit of a good it exports. |
| Countervailing duty | A duty an importing country levies specifically against subsidized imports. |
| Deadweight loss | The portion of a restriction's welfare cost that is a pure efficiency loss, not a transfer between groups. |
| Regional trading bloc (RTA) | A group of countries that has agreed to reduce and progressively eliminate trade (and, in deeper forms, factor-mobility) barriers among its members. |
| Free trade area (FTA) | A bloc eliminating barriers to goods/services trade among members, with each member keeping its own external policy. |
| Customs union | An FTA plus a common external trade policy toward non-members. |
| Common market | A customs union plus free movement of factors of production among members. |
| Economic union | A common market plus common economic institutions and coordinated economic policy. |
| Monetary union | An economic union plus a shared common currency. |
| Trade creation | Regional integration replacing higher-cost domestic production with lower-cost imports from a member. |
| Trade diversion | Regional integration replacing lower-cost imports from a non-member with higher-cost imports from a member. |

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Confusing comparative advantage (relative opportunity cost) with absolute advantage (raw productivity) — a country can lack every absolute advantage and still gain from trade.
- Treating "gains from trade" as meaning every stakeholder benefits, rather than that winners could in theory compensate losers.
- Assuming a quota automatically matches a tariff's welfare outcome — it only ties the tariff if the rent is captured domestically (for example, via license auction); otherwise it is strictly worse.
- Forgetting that a VER's rent goes to the foreign exporter by construction, making it at least as costly to the importing country as a worst-case quota.
- Assuming an export subsidy lowers the domestic price — in the small-country case it raises the domestic price, since producers redirect sales toward the subsidized export market.
- Treating "large country" and "small country" in this chapter as literal size or GDP rankings rather than the price-taker/price-setter distinction actually used.
- Assuming any increase in imports after a customs union forms is trade creation — only the portion that displaces prior domestic production is trade creation; the rest is new consumption.
- Assuming a bloc with free factor mobility automatically has a common external tariff, or vice versa — each integration-ladder feature must be confirmed independently.
- Assuming deeper integration is costless — it constrains independent economic policy, most severely in a monetary union, where devaluation is unavailable and monetary policy is shared.

## Memory aids

- **Five reasons to trade:** exchange and specialization, economies of scale, variety, competition, efficient allocation.
- **Opportunity cost, not raw productivity, decides comparative advantage.**
- **A+B+C+D lost by consumers; A gained by producers; C is a transfer; B+D is gone for good.**
- **Tariff keeps C at home (government); quota's C is ambiguous; VER's C always leaves (to the foreign exporter).**
- **Export subsidy: price up, production up, consumption down, exports up, government spending up (not revenue).**
- **The integration ladder, in order: FTA → Customs Union → Common Market → Economic Union → Monetary Union.**
- **Trade creation fixes inefficiency; trade diversion causes it.**

## Exam tips

- For welfare-calculation questions, write out ΔP, `Qs_ft`, `Qs_r`, `Qd_r`, `Qd_ft` explicitly before computing any of the four effects — most errors come from mismatching which quantity belongs in which term.
- For restriction-comparison questions, build the government-revenue-effect row first (tariff up, quota mixed, subsidy spending up, VER no effect) — it is the fastest, least ambiguous way to identify which restriction a scenario describes.
- For trading-bloc classification questions, check each integration-ladder feature (external tariff, factor mobility, shared institutions, shared currency) independently rather than assuming they arrive as a bundle.
- For trade creation/diversion questions, isolate the change in *domestic production* specifically — that is trade creation; a switch away from a cheaper non-member source is trade diversion.
- For investment-implications questions, expect to name both an integration benefit (market access, scale) and a limitation (persistent country differences, contagion risk) rather than treating bloc membership as unambiguously positive.

## 30-second summary

Countries gain from trade through exchange and specialization based on comparative advantage, plus economies of scale, product variety, and competition — though the adjustment process imposes real, unevenly distributed costs. Tariffs and quotas raise the domestic price identically, transferring surplus from consumers to producers and (for a tariff, or a quota with domestically captured rent) the government, while destroying part of it as deadweight loss; a VER always sends that captured portion abroad, making it the costliest of the three for the importing country. Export subsidies raise the domestic price and increase government spending rather than revenue while stimulating exports. Trading blocs deepen along a fixed ladder — free trade area, customs union, common market, economic union, monetary union — each level adding a specific new feature, and regional integration creates trade (efficiency gain) and diverts trade (efficiency loss) simultaneously, with deeper integration also constraining members' independent economic policy, most severely inside a monetary union.
