# Working Capital and Liquidity

## Learning outcomes

- explain the cash conversion cycle and compare issuers’ cash conversion cycles
- explain liquidity and compare issuers’ liquidity levels
- describe issuers’ objectives and compare methods for managing working capital and liquidity

## 1. Cash timing and the operating cycle

An issuer commits resources to inventory, sells goods, records receivables, and collects cash. The **operating cycle** measures the inventory and collection stages:

**Operating cycle = days of inventory on hand (DIO) + days sales outstanding (DSO)**

The **cash conversion cycle (CCC)** recognizes supplier financing:

**CCC = DIO + DSO − days payable outstanding (DPO)**

Use average balance-sheet balances with the corresponding period flows when deriving days measures. A shorter cycle often releases cash, but comparisons require similar business models and policies.

### Original operating-cycle flow

| Stage | Metric | Higher value means |
|---|---|---|
| Acquire to sell inventory | DIO | cash is committed longer |
| Sell to collect | DSO | customer cash arrives later |
| Supplier credit | DPO | payment is deferred longer |

### Original operating cycle versus CCC

| Measure | Formula | Supplier financing included? |
|---|---|---|
| Operating cycle | DIO + DSO | No |
| Cash conversion cycle | DIO + DSO − DPO | Yes |

### Worked example 1

North Quay has DIO of 44 days and DSO of 26 days. Its operating cycle is **70 days**.

### Worked example 2

North Quay also has DPO of 31 days. CCC is **44 + 26 − 31 = 39 days**. Supplier credit finances 31 days of its 70-day operating cycle.

### Worked example 3

An issuer cuts DIO from 52 to 45 and DSO from 34 to 30 while DPO stays 28. CCC falls from 58 to 47 days, releasing 11 days of cash commitment.

### Worked example 4

A retailer has DIO 18, DSO 2, and DPO 35. Its operating cycle is 20 days and CCC is **−15 days**. Negative CCC is possible because supplier credit exceeds the inventory-plus-collection period.

### Worked example 5

Issuer A has CCC 25 days and Issuer B 55 days. A converts net operating investment faster, but an analyst must investigate industry, service levels, supplier terms, and seasonality before calling A superior.

### Original cycle-direction dashboard

| Change | Operating cycle | CCC |
|---|---:|---:|
| DIO rises | rises | rises |
| DSO falls | falls | falls |
| DPO rises | unchanged | falls |

### Original cycle-policy trade-offs

| Action | Cash benefit | Possible cost |
|---|---|---|
| Lower inventory | shorter DIO | stockouts |
| Tighten customer terms | shorter DSO | lost sales |
| Extend supplier terms | longer DPO | lost discount or relationship damage |

## 2. Liquidity levels, sources, drags, and pulls

Liquidity is the ability to meet obligations when due without unacceptable loss. The long-run primary source for most issuers is cash flow from operations. Costly crisis responses—asset sales, equity issuance, contract renegotiation, or bankruptcy protection—are secondary sources.

A **drag** reduces or delays cash inflows. A **pull** accelerates outflows or interrupts credit. Ratios add evidence but do not replace analysis of asset quality, cash-flow reliability, liability timing, and funding access.

### Original primary versus secondary sources

| Class | Examples | Signal |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | operating cash flow; sustainable committed financing access | normal capacity |
| Secondary | distressed asset sale; emergency equity; contract renegotiation | stress and cost |

### Original liquidity drag/pull matrix

| Event | Classification | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Customers pay later | Drag | inflow delayed |
| Inventory becomes obsolete | Drag | expected conversion impaired |
| Supplier demands early payment | Pull | outflow accelerated |
| Bank cancels a line | Pull | credit interrupted |

### Original liquidity ratio ladder

| Ratio | Numerator | Breadth |
|---|---|---|
| Current | all current assets | broadest |
| Quick | cash + short-term marketable instruments + receivables | excludes inventory |
| Cash | cash + marketable instruments | narrowest |

These are canonical FSA formulas reused here. The 2027 source explicitly uses the stated quick-ratio numerator.

### Worked example 6

Cash is 20, marketable instruments 10, receivables 40, inventory 50, and current liabilities 80. Current ratio is 1.50, quick ratio is **0.875**, and cash ratio is **0.375**.

### Worked example 7

An issuer’s current ratio is high because slow inventory dominates current assets. Its reported coverage may not translate into timely cash; inventory age and turnover are essential context.

### Worked example 8

A major customer disputes an invoice. This is a drag: expected cash inflow is reduced or delayed. It is not a pull, because no outflow has accelerated.

### Worked example 9

A lender declines to renew a facility just before seasonal inventory purchases. The credit interruption is a pull and can force use of costly secondary sources.

### Worked example 10

Two issuers have identical quick ratios. One has diversified customers and a committed facility; the other has concentrated overdue receivables and near-term debt. The first has stronger practical liquidity despite the equal ratio.

### Liquidity warning-sign dashboard

| Evidence | Warning |
|---|---|
| rising overdue receivables | collection drag |
| growing obsolete inventory | conversion drag |
| shortening supplier terms | payment pull |
| expiring facilities | funding pull |

## 3. Managing working capital and liquidity

Management seeks operating continuity and timely payment while controlling financing cost and low-return asset holdings. A conservative policy holds larger buffers and uses more stable financing; an aggressive policy holds smaller buffers and relies more on short-term financing; a moderate policy balances them.

### Original policy spectrum

| Policy | Liquidity buffer | Stable funding | Risk | Opportunity cost |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Conservative | high | high | lower | higher |
| Moderate | medium | medium | medium | medium |
| Aggressive | low | low | higher | lower |

### Original receivables decision table

| Decision | Likely benefit | Likely risk |
|---|---|---|
| tighten standards | fewer bad debts, lower DSO | lost sales |
| loosen terms | sales support | higher DSO and credit loss |
| offer early-pay discount | faster collection | discount cost |

### Original inventory decision table

| Decision | Likely benefit | Likely risk |
|---|---|---|
| reduce safety stock | lower DIO/carrying cost | stockouts |
| build safety stock | service resilience | obsolescence/cash commitment |

### Original payables strategy matrix

| Action | Immediate liquidity | Longer-term effect |
|---|---|---|
| take valuable discount | cash leaves sooner | procurement saving |
| use agreed full term | preserves cash | maintains relationship |
| pay beyond terms | preserves cash briefly | damages credit and supply |

### Original funding maturity map

| Need | Better-aligned funding |
|---|---|
| temporary seasonal build | short-term facility |
| persistent working-capital base | stable longer-term funding |
| unexpected stress | contingency sources |

### Scenario comparison

| Scenario | Cycle effect | Liquidity interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| faster collections | DSO and CCC fall | inflow improves |
| leaner inventory | DIO and CCC fall | cash improves; watch stockouts |
| lost supplier credit | DPO falls, CCC rises | pull on liquidity |

### Worked example 11

A firm tightens customer standards and expects DSO to fall 8 days but sales to fall 3%. The cash benefit must be compared with lost contribution margin; minimizing DSO is not the objective by itself.

### Worked example 12

A supplier offers 2% off for payment on day 10 instead of day 40. Management should compare the discount’s implicit return with financing cost and the value of retained liquidity.

### Worked example 13

Reducing safety stock releases cash and lowers DIO, but expected stockout costs rise. The correct policy minimizes total economic cost rather than inventory alone.

### Worked example 14

A seasonal retailer funds a three-month inventory build with a revolving line, while financing its permanent working-capital base with longer-term capital. This maturity matching reduces rollover pressure.

### Worked example 15

Factoring receivables provides cash immediately but charges a fee and may affect customer relationships. It improves near-term liquidity only after recognizing those costs and whether credit risk transfers.

## Compact revision sheet

Operating cycle is DIO + DSO; CCC is operating cycle − DPO. Higher DIO/DSO lengthens both cycles, while higher DPO shortens only CCC. Sustainable operating cash flow is the principal long-run liquidity source. Delayed inflows are drags; accelerated outflows or interrupted credit are pulls. Conservative, moderate, and aggressive policies trade resilience against financing and carrying costs.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- Do not subtract DPO when calculating the operating cycle.
- Do subtract DPO when calculating CCC.
- Use average balances for days ratios when required and align periods.
- Do not infer liquidity from one ratio or assume shorter CCC is always better.
- Classify delayed/reduced inflows as drags and accelerated outflows/interrupted credit as pulls.
- Do not duplicate canonical FSA formulas; apply them to issuer decisions.
- Keep LM4 focused on short-term operating assets, liabilities, and liquidity—not capital allocation or WACC.

## 30-second summary

The operating cycle connects inventory and receivables; CCC subtracts supplier financing. Liquidity depends on sustainable sources, usable assets, obligation timing, and access to funding. Sound working-capital management balances cash efficiency with sales, service, supplier relationships, funding risk, and profitability.
