# Business Models

## Learning outcomes

- describe key features of business models
- describe various types of business models

## 30-second summary

A business model explains whom a firm serves, what value it offers, where it reaches customers, how it prices, and how its resources, processes, suppliers, and partners deliver that value. Analysts independently test whether these pieces reinforce one another and whether the resulting revenues, costs, scalability, dependencies, and risks are sustainable.

## 1. Key features: who, what, where, how much, and how

### Original business-model canvas

| Question | Evidence to seek | Principal risk |
|---|---|---|
| Who? | Segments, needs, concentration | A narrow or misunderstood customer base |
| What and why? | Offering and value proposition | Weak differentiation |
| Where? | Direct, intermediary, digital, physical channels | Conflict or inaccessible customers |
| How much? | Price architecture and willingness to pay | Price/value mismatch |
| How? | Resources, value chain, suppliers, partners | Capacity or dependency failure |

The value proposition is the bundle of attributes that makes a customer choose the offering. The value chain is the firm’s internal system for creating that value; the supply chain extends across internal and external parties that produce and deliver it.

### Worked example 1 — Subscription fitness service

A service targets time-poor professionals, offers guided workouts, distributes through an app, and charges monthly. The components are coherent if continued engagement supports renewal and digital delivery lets the customer base grow faster than coaching cost.

### Value proposition/customer map

| Segment | Job to be done | Valued attribute | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Busy professional | Exercise consistently | Convenience | Use and renewal |
| Beginner | Learn safely | Guidance | Completion and support data |
| Enthusiast | Improve performance | Depth | Upgrade behavior |

### Worked example 2 — Customer concentration

A component maker earns 55% of revenue from one customer. Efficient account service is a strength, but the relationship is also a material dependency: loss, repricing, or insourcing by that buyer can overwhelm gains elsewhere.

### Original customer-concentration risk matrix

| Concentration | Switching alternatives | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Low | Many | Diversified and replaceable |
| High | Many | Concentrated but potentially recoverable |
| Low | Few | Broad demand with constrained access |
| High | Few | Acute dependency |

### Worked example 3 — Asset-light designer

A product designer owns patents and customer relationships but contracts manufacturing. It avoids large factories and may scale quickly, yet supplier quality, capacity, intellectual-property protection, and bargaining power remain central risks.

### Original asset-light versus asset-heavy comparison

| Feature | Asset-light | Asset-heavy |
|---|---|---|
| Owned capacity | Limited | Substantial |
| Fixed investment | Usually lower | Usually higher |
| Control | More partner-dependent | More direct |
| Scaling constraint | Partner/network capacity | Owned capacity and utilization |

### Worked example 4 — Pricing power

A specialist software tool prevents costly errors and is deeply embedded in customer workflows. Its differentiation and switching friction may support pricing power; an analyst still tests alternatives, renewal behavior, and whether price increases damage retention.

### Worked example 5 — Omnichannel retailer

A retailer coordinates stores, delivery, and online pickup. The model adds convenience only if inventory, pricing, returns, and customer information work across channels; disconnected systems create cost and frustration.

### Original operating-model overview

```text
Resources → internal value chain → channel → customer outcome
    ↑              ↑                  ↑             ↓
 suppliers      capabilities       partners      feedback
```

### Worked example 6 — Scalability

A digital education provider incurs high initial content and platform costs but low cost for another learner. It is potentially scalable, not automatically profitable: acquisition, support, platform reliability, and content renewal can still rise with users.

### Original scalability matrix

| Incremental delivery cost | Capacity replication | Scalability |
|---|---|---|
| Low | Easy | High potential |
| Low | Difficult | Bottleneck risk |
| High | Easy | Growth requires resources |
| High | Difficult | Low potential |

## 2. Revenue, pricing, channels, and model types

Conventional models include manufacturing, distribution, retailing, direct sales, reselling, licensing, franchising, and contract manufacturing. Innovation frequently recombines these with subscriptions, transaction fees, advertising, platforms, marketplaces, crowdsourcing, freemium access, and digital channels.

### Original revenue-model comparison

| Mechanism | Revenue trigger | Central question |
|---|---|---|
| Subscription | Periodic access | Will customers renew? |
| Transaction fee | Completed exchange | Can volume and trust grow? |
| Advertising | Audience access | Is attention valuable and durable? |
| Licensing | Right to use IP/brand | Is the right protected? |
| Product sale | Transfer of product | Are demand and repeat purchases durable? |

### Worked example 7 — Transaction platform

A payment platform receives a fee when merchants and consumers transact. More participants can strengthen usefulness, but fraud, trust, participant concentration, bypass, and the cost of maintaining both sides constrain the opportunity.

### Original recurring-revenue spectrum

```text
One-time sale ── repeat purchase ── usage contract ── renewable subscription
lower visibility                                      higher visibility
```

### Worked example 8 — Advertising model

A free local-information service attracts users and sells targeted placements. Its paying customer is the advertiser, while user attention is the resource being monetized. Privacy rules, weak engagement, and advertiser concentration are key risks.

### Original pricing-model comparison

| Pricing approach | Logic | Typical risk |
|---|---|---|
| Premium | Superior perceived value | Differentiation disappoints |
| Penetration | Sacrifice margin to build scale | Price cannot later rise |
| Tiered | Segment by volume/features | Confusing or unfair tiers |
| Dynamic | Respond to demand/capacity/time | Customer backlash |
| Freemium | Free basic, paid enhanced | Too few users convert |
| Bundle | Combine offerings | Hidden weak components |

### Worked example 9 — Licensing

A studio permits a manufacturer to use a character brand for a royalty. Licensing can be asset-light, but revenue depends on partner execution, contract enforcement, brand protection, and the continuing appeal of the intellectual property.

### Worked example 10 — Franchise

A restaurant group supplies brand, operating processes, and purchasing arrangements to independently owned outlets. Franchise capital can accelerate growth, while inconsistent service, franchisee economics, and monitoring threaten the shared brand.

### Original conventional/unconventional model map

| Model | Primary contribution | Common dependency |
|---|---|---|
| Contract manufacturer | Production | Customer programs |
| Reseller | Distribution/augmentation | Upstream supplier |
| Franchise | Replicable format and brand | Franchisee execution |
| Platform | Participant coordination | Network liquidity |
| Crowdsourcing | User contribution | Quality and participation |

### Worked example 11 — Razor and consumables

A water-filter company prices the durable dispenser aggressively and earns margin on replacement cartridges. Attractive economics require a sufficiently large installed base, continuing cartridge use, and defenses against compatible substitutes.

### Original business-model risk map

| Component | Failure question |
|---|---|
| Customer | Is the segment large, reachable, and retained? |
| Offering | Is value differentiated and defensible? |
| Channel | Can it acquire and serve economically? |
| Pricing | Does willingness to pay exceed delivery cost? |
| Operations | Can quality and capacity scale? |
| Partners | Are critical relationships replaceable? |

### Worked example 12 — Hybrid model diagnosis

A collaboration service offers free individual accounts, paid team subscriptions, and transaction fees on integrated purchases. It is a hybrid: the analyst separately tests conversion, renewal, transaction activity, cross-subsidy, service cost, and whether the mechanisms reinforce rather than cannibalize one another.

## Common mistakes and exam tips

- Do not confuse value chain with the broader supply chain.
- A revenue model states how money is earned; a pricing model states how the charge is determined.
- Asset-light does not mean risk-free, and recurring does not mean guaranteed.
- A platform coordinates participants; crowdsourcing specifically uses participant contributions.
- Analyze consistency among components instead of memorizing a management label.

## Revision sheet

Start with who, what/why, where, how much, and how. Then identify the revenue trigger, pricing logic, resources, partners, channels, scalability, concentration, and contradictions. Finish by asking what must remain true for customer value and issuer economics to persist.
